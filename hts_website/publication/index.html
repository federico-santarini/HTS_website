The Holistic Technology Salon

The Holistic Technology Salon (HTS) is a living collective research space for co-creating alternative technological futures through multidimensional conversations that integrate diverse ways of knowing. Throughout 2023-2024, HTS organized gatherings and events in which artists, designers and researchers shared their knowledge with the community. The Holistic Technology Salon was created to counter the current conception of the technosphere as an autonomous, abstract domain divorced from ecological and social realities. The theoretical and ontological basis for HTS is to challenge this dominant perception, advocating for a relational understanding of technology as part of a living, entangled system that includes humans, non-human entities, and the environment. In response to a lack of holistic understanding of the technosphere, HTS asks: How can we relate to the technosphere as an integral part of the planetary experience from a care-centric perspective?

Since 2023, HTS has developed through salons, workshops, performances, artistic commissions and interdisciplinary collaborations between artists, designers, researchers, technologists and audiences. Inspired by the historical salon as a space for exchange, HTS has evolved into a dynamic artistic research platform that weaves different ways of producing knowledge.

The Methodology

The Triskele is the inspiration for the artistic research methodology that guides HTS. The ancient Celtic symbol of three interconnected spirals represents our research as a living ecology, in which different forms of knowledge remain distinct, while continuously informing each other in a mutually beneficial way. The Triskel proposes a model of artistic research that includes PERCEPTION, COGNITION and RELATIONALITY.

PERCEPTION explores embodied knowledge through workshops, movement, ritual, making and participatory encounters, inviting participants to contribute directly to the evolution of the research through their lived experience.

COGNITION materializes through reading groups, writing, editorial processes and collaborations with academic researchers and writers.

RELATIONALITY relates to artist conversations, public discussions, interviews, radio broadcasts and collective reflection, recognising conversation, both prompted and spontaneous, as a generative space for expanding knowledge.

Together, these three spirals shape our artistic research methodology as a diverse and living space that we collectively hold. We see every person who has participated, encountered or engaged with HTS as part of its community.

How to Read

Not unlike the holistic approach that the Holistic Technology Salon has been striving to apply and embody in the artistic field, this publication strives to connect projects and artefacts together, to see if they react to each other, what relationships they create, and what they teach us about themselves.

The method that we, as Holistic Technology Salon, have developed, or better, discovered, is represented in this book as the intertwined spirals of the Celtic Triskele symbol.

The Triskele is a triad that makes a whole. The HTS cohort is a triad, also making a whole. Our research and artistic methodology is a triad making a whole: PERCEPTION, COGNITION, and RELATIONALITY, which together make our attempt at a holistic approach.

A holistic approach is not a homogenising one. The difference between each artwork or text adds to it. Each author has a different style, tone, and way of organising thinking. Each has a focus, a method. Whether it is more connected to perception, to cognition, or to relationality, when meeting in the pages of this publication, they become particles of a whole, elements contributing to a shared understanding. .

As the Triskele represents and symbolically guides us in the realization of a holistic approach to technology, we leave this publication to exist in a trifold state. A reader approaching it might feel compelled to read into each contribution as belonging to one of the three categories. We invite you to catch, in each work, perhaps a tendency for one of those categories to present itself more strongly, and at the same time see it as part of a mechanism, an activity being performed by multiple agents at the same time.

The content of this publication is therefore ‘organized’ around these three spirals, each contributing to this wholeness. As everything, these will shift with time, with interlocutors, with feelings, and many other factors. The written word binds us to cognition somehow. Therefore, on a meta-level this e-publication has also been organized around the moving spirals of the Triskele, towards PERCEPTION. It exists within the exhibition opening at V2_ in November 2026, which organizes itself on the PERCEPTION spiral, and this will be activated through an open program focused on RELATIONALITY.

You might also follow your intuition, opening the book wherever it falls and allowing a particular text, image, or idea to catch your attention. There is no need to follow a linear path; instead, let curiosity guide you through the spirals and discover the connections that emerge along the way.

We embrace the fractality of this whole project and hope to invite participation into this ever-evolving spiral of meaning, that somehow is shaping itself around us.

In Search of Holistic Methods

Dr Evelyn Wan

holistic (adjective)

ho·lis·tic

Relating to or concerned with complete systems rather than with individual parts, especially: concerned with the whole person, animal, or organism as opposed to a specific part or aspect.

— Merriam Webster Dictionary

What does it mean to embody a holistic perspective?

Since the New Age movement in the 1970s, ‘holistic’ has often been understood as the mind-body-spirit triad. Holistic medicine, like traditional Chinese medicine and Ayurveda, considers the body as a whole system and uses medical interventions to readjust the entire body’s equilibrium. This is based on the view that the body has innate intelligence that can heal itself through the ‘life force’ (e.g. chi/qi, or prana) that animates the whole body. In Western medicine, a holistic approach can mean studying the patient through the tripartite bio-physio-social structure, that evaluates health outcomes based on biological factors like pathogens, as well as psychological and social dimensions. In systems thinking, a holistic perspective entails studying emergent properties within a system, characteristics which only appear when the system functions as a whole.

This interplay between parts and wholes is not unique to systems science, and extends into how we interpret the world and understand its meaning. In hermeneutics, the branch of knowledge on interpretation of literary or religious texts, we refer to semantic holism, where words only have meaning through the context of a whole language. This is often explained through the ‘hermeneutic circle’: to understand something on the whole you need to understand its parts, but to understand the parts, you also need the whole.

However, modern science and philosophy has often broken the circle of holism. In our pursuit of objectivity, we decided that we actually do not need to understand the whole in order to understand a part. The part is sometimes enough. A case-in-point here is the Cartesian mind/ body split. Descartes’s adage “Cogito ergo sum/ I think therefore I am” suggests that the mind is the privileged site of reason and logic, and the body is constantly swept to the side. We still live in the aftermath of this hierarchisation in academia. As scholars, we effectively learn to ignore our gut feelings, intuitions, and embodied reactions in the search for rational argumentations and objectivity — logos above pathos, reasoning over feeling, belief, and intuition.

Artistic practice, which gives space for full-bodied presence and sensorial engagement, becomes an important partner for rebuilding such knowledge links to the self and to the myriad expressiveness of the body. As a dance practitioner, I train my intuitive body for improvisation, and I lean into my gut feelings. Being in flow when dancing brings me into an altered state of consciousness, where I feel a heightened connection to fellow dancers and to my environment.11 With a note of appreciation for my dance teachers, Andy Wong (contemporary), Gigi Yiu (jazz), John Kayongo (East African dance), and Suzan Lemont (improvisation), who have transmitted much embodied knowledge through their respective practice. Yet, my sweaty body with all its unbridled sensations rarely entered my academic reflections. Later, as I transitioned from ‘maiden’ to ‘mother’ after giving birth, this other version of my body, the maternal postpartum body of milk, blood, spiritual and existential transformations, also failed to enter scholarly writing, even though I felt like I looked at the world with a new pair of eyes in motherhood.22 I am grateful to the companionship of Dr Anika Marschall who has navigated the deep waters of motherhood together with me as a fellow performance studies scholar. She has also lent her critical eye to this essay and gave me valuable feedback, while on maternity leave, no less. ‘Corporeality’ and ‘embodiment’ might be lovely academic terms, but the actual, breathing body and its inner psyche and soul often disappear in the folds of cognitively-driven research and writing.33 See Ben Spatz (2020) for a discussion of how accounts of embodiment, even within embodied research and performance studies, remain stuck in paradigms of scientism.

In the last few years, with these reflections in mind, I began wondering how I could do scholarship differently. I am currently Assistant Professor in Media, Arts, and Society at Utrecht University4,4 Recently, I discovered that the university used to be State University of Utrecht, and held research books in the deeply colonial discipline of Indologische Studiën (Indological Studies): https://repertorium.library.uu.nl/collectie/indologische-studien/ where I also completed my postgraduate training and PhD in Media and Performance Studies. I relocated there from Hong Kong, my hometown, approximately 15 years ago. At UU, I teach research methods, and am obliged to grade my own students according to their performance of the ‘golden standards’ of academic research. Over the years, I have been disciplined into regarding ‘peer-reviewed science’ as the highest form of knowledge.

When pre-eminent Black Studies scholar Katherine McKittrick published Dear Science and Other Stories (2021), I latched onto it like a lifeline. McKittrick draws from a rich repository of black and anti-colonial methodologies and theoretical frameworks to challenge mainstream academic methods, recasting them as insular, disciplinary, exclusionary, and premised upon the racial logics of European modernity. The book reminded me that science looked different to different communities. Black science, for instance, could take the form of storytelling and music, “across a range of places, times, genres, texts, shadows, grooves” (12) in the lifeworld of black humanity and black livingness. McKittrick’s intervention gave me the push I needed to commit to decolonial work in media studies, to ask what media studies and technologies look like from my own situated knowledge of East Asian cosmology and lifeworld.

In an attempt to define what a non-Eurocentric definition of technology could stand for, I landed upon the lost meaning of ‘medium’ and ‘mediumship’ in media studies, where the mediation of messages from other realms by women, indigenous communities, and people of colour have been conveniently left out of media history.55 John Durham Peters’ Speaking in the Air: A History of the Idea of Communication (1999) is a notable exception, and details mesmerism and spiritualism as a crucial part of the esoteric side of media history. I began looking into the intersections between technology and spirituality both in Europe and in Hong Kong. I researched séances, discovered channelling women in the British Spiritualist archives, and eventually made a performance on such histories.66 See further Wan (2026), which features my archival research and séance performance on English medium Florence Cook (1856-1904). How might stories of technology be told differently, and how might they propose new textures, tales, narratives, and concepts for an expanded field of media and performance studies?

On this journey, I am grateful for the company of Holistic Technology Salon77 Holistic Technology Salon is produced by V2_ Lab for the Unstable Media in Rotterdam, The Netherlands. (“HTS”), where technology meets spirituality meets artistic practice. In HTS, the dimensions of body, spirit, and being that I intimately knew but have always bracketed out found a space to be taken seriously. I have been privy to many activities of HTS, and am proudly a member of the larger expert circle in this network of artists, curators, and researchers. What has often struck me as unconventional is the way HTS works with an expansive definition of technology, centring unconventional themes such as esotericism, spiritual inner technologies, and magic, next to digital tech and AI. We meditated together, mourned the death of devices, questioned our relationship to technologies, played with sensors, embraced love as a technology, stared at the black mirrors of our screens until we entered into trance and saw visions dancing on the edge of our consciousness… We shared our curiosities, our questions, and our desires towards another vision of technology, one that moves beyond what Big Tech dictates as gadgets of the future.

The Holistic Technology Salon has been an interesting space of experimentation, and I have always felt that I could show up with my full embodied self, without severing my body and spirit from my intellectual work. In this essay, I explore further what makes a ‘holistic’ method, ponder how a holistic method dovetails with artistic research, and offer ways to contextualise the Holistic Technology Salon. Via detours and musings, I offer auto-ethnographic accounts88 Here I am indebted to the research and pedagogical work of my colleague Dr Theron Schmidt, who added a session on Embodied Research and Critical Auto-ethnography to the Art and Performance Research Lab course offered jointly across our MA programmes. The Holistic as a Conduit for the Decolonial

To situate the holistic triad of mind-body-spirit in my lifeworld, I begin with a positionality statement. I am a postcolonial subject who grew up in British Hong Kong and witnessed the handover of sovereignty from Great Britain to the People’s Republic of China in 1997. My maternal lineage has roots in French Indochina, while my paternal lineage hails from the southern Chinese coast. As a child and teenager, I went to a Christian kindergarten, then a Catholic primary and secondary school. In our classrooms, we prayed each morning to God, Jesus, or Holy Mary. I studied the Bible from the Old Testament to the New and scored a B in the public examination subject of ‘Religious Studies’ — but this title was a misnomer. Biblical Studies would have been more apt, and this strongly reflects the colonial bias of what counts as legitimate ‘religion’ in Hong Kong.

Outside of school, Buddhist, Taoist, and folk practices are much more common, though sometimes tinged with a sense of backward conservatism and superstition in a cosmopolitan city. My neighbours had a little shrine at their front door where they worshipped the God of Property (土地公) for safe passage and a prosperous home. On the weekends, when I visited my grandparents on an outlying island, I would play outside the Tin Hau Temple (天后廟). Islanders and fisherfolk believe that Tin Hau, the Goddess of the Sea, would keep them safe. In the city, traditional fortune-tellers line the night market, and in my teenage years, my friends would seek advice from Tarot readers tucked away in tiny shops in shopping arcades, eager to see their futures with their crushes and first loves. Every Lunar New Year, extensive predictions and analyses of the year ahead are broadcasted on TV, based on the animals of the Chinese zodiac. At the Ching Ming Festival, we would burn incense to pay respect to our ancestors when we visit their graves.

I grew up in a rich and multicultural spiritual world where competing belief systems co-exist, mostly in harmony. The identity of a modern progressive cosmopolitan subject in Hong Kong, however, privileges Westernisation. It is one thing to partake in traditional rites and rituals, but to truly believe in them? That’s superstition. Or to quote Chicana feminist Gloria Anzaldúa, in her seminal work Borderlands (1987),“I allowed white rationality to tell me that the existence of the ‘other world’ was mere pagan superstition. I accepted their reality, the ‘official reality’ of the rational, reasoning mode, which is connected with external reality” (36-37). Like Anzaldúa, I learned to prefer subjects that would allegedly connect me to the wider world, ones I later discovered simply represented the hegemony of Global North and Anglo-/ Euro-centric ontologies and epistemologies, and severed my connection to my own culture. Ben Spatz, in Blue Sky Body: Thresholds for Embodied Research (2020), refers to academic training as a training in whiteness (36), which makes invisible the violence of erasure where forms of knowledge(s) stemming from whiteness are legitimised over others.

I was a conscientious student, and studied arts and sciences. I embraced the rational, scientific worldview, and consumed Western cultural artefacts from Hollywood to Shakespeare to Van Gogh. I read Western political philosophy and cultural theory at university, majored in government and laws, and studied great juridical minds of the British Commonwealth. In my relocation to the Netherlands to study Gender Studies and Media and Performance Studies at graduate school, I became well-versed too in Continental philosophy; I quote Foucault, Heidegger, Deleuze, and many other dead white men in my work. In fact, I have mastered the coloniser’s tongue so well that I, a woman of colour of mixed Asian descent, am tasked to develop and teach the core course ‘Philosophy of Science in Cultural Inquiry’ in my department’s BA programme.

Yet something shifted fundamentally in me in parallel as I became Other in the Western European99 I generally refer to Western Europe when I evoke the term ‘European’ in this essay. I acknowledge here that Europe is not a monolithic category, and is not coherent internally. The dominant narrative of Western Europe has also subjugated the cultures of the Mediterranean, Nordic cultures, Slavic cultures, Baltic cultures, Celtic cultures etc., which all have their own lively esoteric practices. I use the term Europe here largely as a shorthand for Western Europe, which the Netherlands belongs to. academy — if Europe is so proud of its intellectual legacy and exported it around the world through colonisation, why shouldn’t I be equally proud of mine? After all, East Asian philosophy has a deep and rich repository of ideas that date back millennia, especially in the cosmology of yin-yang (陰陽) and theories of cosmic order that detail the correlative correspondence between heaven, earth, nature, and humanity. In my spare time, I found myself religiously studying the Tao Te Ching (道德經) and the teachings of Buddhist monks. In recent years, as this practice of re-indigenisation consolidated, I studied the I-Ching (The Book of Changes/ 易經) to learn traditional cosmology, picked up Qigong (氣功) to train my body, and immersed myself in the mythology of Chinese gods, goddesses, and mythical beasts that once fascinated me as a child. Since last year, in my determination to relearn my mother tongue, I began to author research-based articles in Chinese too. But this is only one side of decolonising my work.

To me, decolonial work does not only involve a deep re-engagement with my own culture and unlearning of Eurocentrism in my training, but also a proposition of how Western Europe can decolonise itself, in order to unravel the hegemony of Enlightenment values and power structures that have taken root in this part of the world. While it is a necessary move for decolonial scholarship to concentrate on the lifeworlds of the formerly colonised and for these voices to find expression, it is equally crucial to scrutinise how the West has suppressed other worlds within its own borders.

In this respect, I am indebted to the genealogical and somatic analysis of US race-based violence in Resmaa Menakem’s My Grandmother’s Hands (2017). Menakem argues that violence committed within Europe towards fellow white bodies during the medieval period was exported to coloured bodies in the New World. Victimised by the brutality of corporeal punishment from burning at the stake to public hangings in England, those who settled in the New World became victimisers as a trauma response. They saw the subjugation of natives and blacks as an opportunity to finally assert dominance and control, a self-protective response which consolidated into dominant racist white culture in the US. Menakem’s work urgently shows that attention to the body must be integrated into decolonial scholarship, as racialisation and the accompanying racial hierarchy are implicated with somatic histories of trauma.

In my view, Silvia Federici’s study of the European witch-hunts between the 16th and 17th centuries supplements Menakem’s account by providing an additional gendered and esoteric lens. In Caliban and the Witch (2004), Federici argues that the witch-hunts, fueled by beliefs in Christian demonology, fractured European peasant communities to create an opening for increased state control, land privatisation, and capitalist expropriation. The systematic elimination of witches was essential to securing capitalism’s patriarchal control over female bodies for forced reproductive labor. Crucially, killing off these bodies also meant destroying embodied and esoteric knowledge systems of folk practices and witchcraft. Witches were frequently older women who held custodianship over contraceptive methods, herbal medicine, and healing rituals, through spellwork, ritual, and community gatherings. Suppression of these embodied practices cleared the path for Enlightenment rationalism and modern scientific authority. As a result, the witch-hunts can be read as an early instance of European epistemicide of magic and esotericism.

Menakem and Federici’s writings reiterate the subjugation of body and spirit within the Western order, and remind us that we are still living in the wake of racialised and gendered violence inflicted upon enslaved, maimed, and murdered bodies. The marginalised knowledge worlds of women, natives, and blacks must also be reclaimed as legitimate ways of knowing. My contention is that a holistic method bringing together mind, body, and spirit will help us decentralize a version of ‘science’ built upon colonial hierarchies and narrow ‘rational’ ideals. My drive to develop holistic methods, therefore, is to build knowledge spaces that enable access to oppressed worlds beyond the mind-centred focus of conventional research. How might such holistic methods be activated, especially within the creative and embodied modalities of artistic research and practice?

Zooming into the esoteric and the artistic

Here I turn my attention once again to the practices of HTS. Even though HTS does not use the concept of ‘decolonisation’ in its self-representations, I view the esoteric and magical nature of its work as crucial to decolonising Europe from within. Central to this effort is the somatic and spiritual body in HTS’s artistic practice. By placing visitors in esoteric and magical worlds, HTS reveals a side of Europe rarely brought under the spotlight and challenges post-Enlightenment rationality by asking visitors to surrender scientific ways of knowing and sink instead into the visions, dreams, and the deep sensations of the material body.

Esotericism takes centre stage in HTS’s work, a testimony to the fact that despite years of erasure via witch-hunts, colonisation, and the subjugation of bodily and spiritual knowledge(s) beyond the rational, esoteric practices have both survived in and outside of Europe.1010 Postcolonial theorist Dipesh Chakrabarty, in his seminal essay ‘Provincializing Europe’ (2000), has already argued that gods and other so-called ‘superstitions’ have actually never died anywhere, even in the heart of Europe itself. Cultural historian Jason Ananda Josephson Storm, in The Myth of Disenchantment (2017), has further established that while science seems to have displaced magic through a process of disenchantment, and magic has excited from the so-called “law-governed world” (3), this process of secularisation has actually never succeeded, even in the ascent of European science. Yet, this begs the question: why has Europe abandoned and dismissed magical forms of knowing in the first place? Silvia Federici would argue that in the transition to modernity, magic was seen to be at odds with a scientific, rationalistic, and capitalist worldview. Magic’s animist view of nature resisted capitalist extraction, while its reliance on unpredictable cosmic forces and fortune disrupted the belief in rigid work discipline required by capitalist labour.

It is perhaps no coincidence that HTS is a collective mainly of feminists and gender non-conforming artists, banded together like a coven of the descendants of witches they failed to eliminate centuries ago. The founding trio, Sieta van Horck, Ginevra Petrozzi, and Annika Kappner, in fact met at a V2_ community event featuring the work of artist Danae Tapia, who runs the Digital Witchcraft Institute.1111 See https://digitalwitchcraft.works/ for more information about the work of Danae Tapia and the Digital Witchcraft Institute. It was also at this very event that I first encountered the artistic practices of Annika Kappner and Ginevra Petrozzi, who both gave guest appearances during Tapia’s presentation.

From an art historical perspective, the artistic practices of the key artists in HTS sit in a niche between technology and spirituality, and often reflect upon realms of magic, divination, mysticism, meditation, and bodily attunement, as well as the impact of technology on society. Their work have been part of museums, biennales, and festivals such as Ars Electronica, INDEX Braga Biennial, IMPAKT, Sonic Acts, MU Hybrid Art House, Het Hem, Het Nieuwe Instituut, and Stedelijk Museum Amsterdam.

In fact, themes of the magical, esoteric, and spiritual have seen a recent resurgence in curatorial and study interest in the Netherlands and around the world. Two successive editions of the Venice Biennale in 2022 and 2024 explicitly included women artists of the occult, indigenous artists, and spirit mediums, where a selection of curated works emphasised alternative cosmologies, ancestral rites, earth-bound spiritualities, rituals, and magic. The 13th Gwangju Biennale in 2021 was themed Minds Rising, Spirits Attuning, and centred Korean shamanism as a key entry-point. The 2026 edition of Transmediale, By the Mango Belt & Tamarind Road, opened with a ritual walk and a cleansing rite with smoke and drum by two artists of African and Asian descent. In the Netherlands, students at the Royal Academy of Art (KABK) chose the theme Wxtch Craft for their Studium Generale programme in 2020-2021, showcasing magic, spellwork, and queer-feminist witchcraft as a contemporary feminist liberatory practice of storytelling, self and community care, and survival. Kunstinstituut Melly, in 84 STEPS (2021-2023), incorporated a diverse public education programme on contemporary spiritual practices alongside their exhibition, which focused on somatic care and holistic healing. IMPAKT Festival Techno-Ancestrality (2026) heavily featured Winti spirituality as a technology of colonial resistance in Suriname. These are mere samplings of a larger movement in the art world that increasingly draws on indigenous cosmologies, ritual practices, and ancestral knowledges,1212 This may also be read as practices of inclusion and broadening the definition of artistic practice and vocabulary beyond the West. often also recast as forms of technology.

In October 2025, I made a dedicated research trip to the Seoul MediaCity Biennale, themed Séance: Technology of the Spirit. Before I saw the exhibition, I was warned by an Asian critic who said, “You know... there’s a certain white bias in the exhibition. White, European curators don’t have enough knowledge of East Asian esoteric practices to really situate this exhibition in Seoul. It feels like the curators fell short dealing with this theme through an overemphasis on artworks from the West.” While it is a fair critique, this comment also fails to acknowledge the continuous resurgence of esotericism in Europe, and the exhibition precisely captures this lesser known history in a genealogy that included the West as well as other cultures. I entered the exhibition through Rudolf Steiner’s blackboard drawings (1920-1924) that anchored the Anthroposophy movement in Switzerland. British spiritualist Georgiana Houghton’s watercolours that captured her channeling of God, saints, and angels lined up behind glass panels. Abstract paintings of Swedish mystic Hilma af Klimt, the mother of abstract art, were steps away from Nam June Paik's TV Buddha (1974), which brought East Asian philosophy and shamanism to the then-burgeoning field of new media art. The exhibition also assembled various non-Western and indigenous artists from many corners of the earth: Karrabing Film Collective shared Aboriginal worldviews through The Family and the Zombie (2021), Jane Jin Kaisen captured shapeshifting nature spirits Dokkaebi (2024) on Jeju island, and Kurdish artist Hiwa K was commissioned to produce They call him Haji Laser (2025), a film installation which focused on a traditional healer who had ‘laser vision’, and could identify tumours and shatter kidney stones with his eyes.

The exhibited works served as a genealogical study of the role spiritual experience has played in the development of modern and contemporary art, mapping an art history that has always been decidedly Western-centric, while expanding the diversity of exhibited works as one ventured deeper into the galleries. Despite rooting the curated journey in Western fascinations with mysticism and spiritualism, the exhibition successfully shows how artists, in and outside of Europe, have long resisted the Enlightenment ideal of ‘rationality’, and have consistently made space for other parallel worlds where the spiritual meets the other-worldly, where ghosts, spirits, and ancestors reside, and anticolonial, feminist, ecological, and anti-capitalist ideas thrive.

Technology of the spirit

To the curators of Seoul MediaCity Biennale, the curated works “widen the aperture of reality to include those forces, entities, and knowledge(s) denied by the dominant ways of ordering and indexing the world” (17). And through this, they develop the concept of “technology of the spirit”. To me, this connects very well to Arturo Escobar’s concept of the ‘pluriverse’ (2020), stemming from his work in the Latin American decolonial school. Escobar argues that parallel worlds have always existed in addition to the capitalist, extractive, colonial world, yet that one world has occupied so much of our existence and imagination that it might be seen as a form of ‘one-worldism’, a universalised, linear progressive way of being in the world. Much like my own experience of growing up in Hong Kong, parallel folk cultural worlds never disappeared despite the spread of Christianity that accompanied colonial modernity. And even in the heart of Western Europe, other belief systems have co-existed and persisted despite violent oppression and structural elimination, such as the witch-hunts I discussed above.

HTS develops a similar idea of the technology of the spirit. Through three key pillars, the collective develops their practice through leaning into the ‘relational’, the ‘cognitive’ and the ‘ultra-sensorial’. The ‘relational’ emphasises that the world has always been relational and interconnected, and invites us to recognise the ecological relations we as humans are embedded in. The ‘relational’ pillar proposes care-centric and eco-centric ways of being together. The ‘cognitive’ attends to computational and machinic logic and explores its alternatives, while incorporating cutting-edge science in quantum physics, material physics and other domains of research as sites of experimentation. The ‘ultra-sensorial’ leans into human sensing through expanded consciousness and retrained attention through modalities such as meditation and yoga, or through machinic sensors and human-machine feedback loops that augment our ability to feel. These intertwining pillars are materialised through artworks, aesthetic experiences, rituals, critical discourse, dialogue, and other happenings that bring audience members into the fold through shared experience in the here and now.1313 With credit to Sieta van Horck, Annika Kappner, and Ginevra Petrozzi, who have articulated and elaborated on their methodologies across multiple events, including Relating, Sensing, Being: Conversations on Arts x Science x AI, hosted at Utrecht University (27 May 2026).

The work of the collective departs from the recognition that the world as we know it is often filtered through complex interlocking systems of power and hegemony imposed upon us by capitalism, organised religion, and other dominant structures. To even notice these filters, we must go via the experiential realm to re-attune ourselves and reclaim our bodies and our attention away from these oppressive structures that have become the norm. Even though HTS does not announce itself as a decolonial practice, it does offer a critical access point into the experiential and the somatic via the artistic. This access point is a crucial first step, a window into what has been suppressed collectively, in the subjugation of esoteric knowledge(s) that is part and parcel of colonial modernity. The holistic becomes a stand-in for resistance, against power-over structures, against the coloniality of knowledge, and provides an opportunity to tell different stories about our relationality to earth and ecology and to one another.1414 This is particularly important for Western Europe, to recover cosmologies and ancestral cultures, witchcraft and otherwise, that have always included a much more diverse worldview. As Jessica Fern writes in Polysecure (2020), it is the obvious lack of ritual and awareness in the contemporary Western milieu which has led to the psychedelic renaissance, “when Westerners travel to South and Central America and partake in ayahuasca rituals for the purposes of healing and self-discovery” (219). Similarly, white people eat, pray, love their way into ashrams and spiritual retreats in India, which accompanies the mass neoliberalisation of yoga practices that are whitewashed and clad in Lululemon athleisure. Only by addressing marginalised esoteric aspects of European history can the West reconnect with the spirituality that has been severed within their own cultural heritages and stop the appropriation of non-white spiritual practices.

In alignment with diverse magical practices that ultimately invite other ways of relating to nature and to ourselves as part of nature, HTS invites re-integration of spiritual and bodily practices as part of their vision of holism, through featuring them in their artistic works and discourse. While the circle of HTS is predominantly composed of white European artists, the larger sphere of inspiration includes a much wider range of reference points from the rest of the world. Just to name a few—Sieta van Horck brought in Sand Talk: How Indigenous Thinking can Save the World (2020) to discuss the notion of a ‘right relationship’ to technology via Aboriginal thinker Tyson Yunkaporta; artist and healer Jihae Ko’s Planetary Acupuncture project investigates Korean traditional acupuncture medicine as a technology of healing the self and the world; V2_ director Fabian Saavedra Lara brought in his experience of Amazonian cultures in Latin America as a German-Chilean in one of our expert meetings; and Annika Kappner and I often discuss the cosmology of yin and yang stemming from East Asian cultures. This inter-referential mode of building HTS does not tend towards a totalising vision of what the esoteric and the magical is, but remains open to the pluriversal worlds the collective inhabits. And these pluriversal worlds open up conduits into a decolonial potentiality.

Living the Holistic Method

HTS enfolds the three pillars of their work, the ‘relational’, the ‘cognitive’ and the ‘ultra-sensorial’, into the artistic as ‘direct experience’ in active spectatorship. HTS’s insistence on ‘direct experience’ suggests that embodied experience is a crucial part of the holistic method. This method is not an abstract scientific proposal that only lives in the discursive, but one that is materially lived and, quite literally, breathed. This insistence on embodied experience emphasises that the corporeal realm must be taken seriously as part of knowledge production, against the constant erasure of bodily knowledge that remains under-theorised. Part of the collective’s work is to develop articulations and choreographies of experience such that what lies beneath the surface of the perceptible world and under our skin can be brought to attention. How can we, through artistic intervention, expand our embodied ways of sensing and being in the world?

This exploration is in line with the model of somatics and transformative justice Staci Haines outlines in her book The Politics of Trauma (2019). Haines explains her view on somatics in the following quotation:

Somatics is fascinated with embodiment—what is embodied, and is that working? Do we have the embodied options we want for love, boundaries, dealing with conflict in a life-affirming way, equity, and more? Somatics looks for aliveness—what allows for more life, more connection, more wisdom, more effective action, and relations? Somatics looks to increase choices—based in agency and empowerment—to ‘becoming’ more individually and collectively whole and skilled at living. (36)

To Haines, somatics is a field that needs to be coupled with social and economic analysis. Through the body, we can gain deeper access to ourselves and develop self-knowledge. With this awareness, we can instigate change and transform our ways of being and relating, such that we can embody transformation, both individually and collectively. This is in alignment with Menakem’s intervention in My Grandmother’s Hands. Beyond the genealogical and somatic analysis of race-based violence, Menakem interjects his theoretical discussions with practical somatic exercises, inviting the reader to sense their bodies as well as conduct various meditations and body and breath practices. To him, it is crucial that the somatic practice is integrated with the cognitive discussion of trauma, and the reader is warned not to skip over these parts and to commit to them.

In fact, in the spirit of the somatic and the holistic, take a few deep breaths now. Notice the sensations that arise in your body. Allow yourself to pause and digest the points raised in the essay so far. Feel free to make yourself a cup of tea before you continue reading. I have had to take multiple breaks from writing this essay, as I sat with the density and diversity of the materials (thoughts, concepts, methods, intuitions, bodily sensations, affects, experiences) assembled over the last few years of my life. I allowed my body to dictate the rhythm of writing, re-writing, and editing, slowly integrating various aspects of my experience into a coherent narrative. In allowing the body to speak, I become more present and connected to the thoughts, sensations and emotions of my body. In becoming more present, I open up deeper possibilities of relational connection with other people—in this case, through channeling my somatic lifeworld into words on a page.

From a somatic perspective, the body is not only the medium but also the technology for social change. Through specific modes of somatic training, Haines suggests that the body can become a vehicle for social change through its insistence on aliveness — how might we feel more? How might we experience more deeply? How might we, through such experiences, build more capacity for further action? While both Haines and Menakem focus on somatic trauma processing as a key training, I would consider a wider field of practices, especially in the arts and in spiritual practice, that work on the somatic body and extend its capacities. From dance and choreography to improvisation, from breathwork to qigong, these disciplines refine our capacity to perceive, process, and embody experience. Creative and contemplative movement honours the body as a dynamic site of knowledge generation. Dancers, meditators, and somatic practitioners alike learn to navigate internal micro-shifts, such as attuning to subtle variations in tone, breath, and spatial awareness. In these modes of practice, the body is not just a medium for moving our very engaged minds into meeting rooms and workspaces. Rather, it is possible to view the body beyond its capacity to labour and produce for capitalist extraction. Attention can be directed to sensory processing and our embodied presence in the world. Through creative and contemplative practices, the body is recognised as an active, expressive agent, capable of affecting others and being affected.

But what exactly are these capabilities? What is a body capable of and what are our bodies collectively capable of? As two dead white male philosophers, Baruch Spinoza and later Gilles Deleuze, famously quipped, “no one knows what a body can do”. To build a holistic method, the body and the spirit should be brought back into research. Methods determine a researcher’s way of navigating the field, and I wonder how direct, embodied, somatic experience might be recorded, narrated, and analysed.

An auto-ethnographic research interlude

In Spring of 2025, I took it upon myself to deepen my field research into séances and folk esoteric practices during a research stint back home in Hong Kong. I was determined to reclaim my own knowledge of a Chinese cosmological world I have been severed from, and unlearn the coloured lens of superstition I have long carried. Through friends of friends, I was invited to visit a spirit-writing group (fuluan 扶鸞 or fuji 扶乩), which has been operational in Hong Kong since 1951. Spirit-writing refers to the (often Taoist) divination practice where messages from deities, ancestors, or spirits, are conveyed through a wooden stylus guided by a medium. The stylus is held by the medium and assistants, and it would move to write words in sand. It originated as a rural women’s practice involving spirit possession of physical objects, before evolving into a more sophisticated written form of spirit communication, where educated, literate practitioners receive messages from historically revered figures and deities from beyond. These spirit-writers consulted spirits on a wide variety of questions, from personal advice to medical prescriptions to broader social and political commentary. According to sociologists of religion, they deliberately avoided dramatic manifestations of trance and spirit possession, so as to distinguish themselves from rural village shamans.1515 See further Lang and Ragvald (1998) for an overview of the development of spirit-writing in Chinese culture.

The history of the spirit-writing group I visited has been exceptionally well-recorded, in part due to the participation of various professors and researchers from local universities. Amongst them are philosophers, researchers, traditional Chinese medical doctors, journalists, and other highly-educated professionals. It turns out that more of us live with double-identities between science and esotericism. I am definitely not alone.1616 The assistant on the day who held the spirit-writing stylus turned out to be my undergraduate philosophy professor who taught me in the course “Critical Thinking and Logic". The medium herself is a retired Chinese literature professor, who has advanced linguistic ability to mediate the deity’s expressions. The deity they contact, affectionately known as Teacher, communicates in classical poetic verses, an old style used by literary elites of centuries past. Born in 1592, he was a scholar who eventually died in army service defending the last days of the Ming Dynasty in the 17th century. At a typical spirit-writing session, the medium poses requests and questions from the followers and receives answers from Teacher. Several scribes would transcribe entire proceedings for record-keeping. It is part spiritual study (in the vein of Taoist and Buddhist philosophy), part trans-temporal dialogue, part worship, and part social gathering.

This field experience was both magical and mundane. The atmosphere in the hall was calm, reverent, and quiet. In hushed breath I listened to every word uttered by the medium, arriving from a place beyond the visible and the sensible. I was struck by the time-bending nature of such a dialogue, and took home various published records the group assembled over the years, to continue studying wise words from the Teacher.

In the evening after the spirit-writing session, I was sitting in a restaurant for dinner when my right arm began trembling uncontrollably. I felt the innate urge to pick up a pen, as if I had to write something down. But I had chopsticks in hand, and dumplings were waiting, and I wasn’t sure if I wanted to find out what the trembling was all about. A few days later, the trembling began again as I read a book-length analysis on spirit-writing for research. Deep in my body, I intuitively knew what this meant, and yet I wanted to resist the meaning and all that it would entail. In time, I steadied myself and let my fear of the unknown dissipate. I answered the call with an incense offering, and readied myself with pen and paper, cautious but curious. I did not know the spirit entity I was connecting with, but I felt a bright white halo around me, tranquil and peaceful. As I allowed the pen to drift aimlessly across the paper, I entered a mild state of trance, and specific phrases floated into my head. I dictated them as they came through, transcribing 4-character phrases in structured Chinese poetic form. I retained my full thinking capacity, and I was half-channeling and half-observing what was going on simultaneously. To my surprise, the entity on the other end reached out to teach me mediumship.

Across two separate trance sessions, it reminded me to have peace in my heart and to still my mind in order to receive messages. It told me that staying in a dream-like state helped with reception, and that my hand would stop writing if I were too afraid. 1717 The channelled message reads “手心神載 / 試煉維新 / 如夢映照 / 身心並用” and “心靜身動 / 動怕不動” in original Chinese. It told me that practice would keep my technique fresh, and eventually invited me to practise weekly in order to develop my clairvoyant talents.1818 I did not continue the practice after two sessions, as I do not intend to become a trained medium.

As I gathered myself after the automatic writing experiences, I began to ponder: what does it mean to answer to a non-human power, or to learn a spiritual technology from beyond this visible and perceptible world? I must be honest here about my experience after this field experience and embodied research. In fact, I did not know what my body was capable of, even after all the somatic trainings as a dancer, even after all the material fluctuations in the transition to motherhood.1919 My body was undergoing strange transformations as part of the process — I had mild nausea after the channelling. For a few days I became a very picky eater, which was out of the ordinary. I could not eat processed food, and rejected heavy flavours, eggs, meat, and dairy products. It was as if I was nudged to go on a cleanse through a light vegan diet, but it also made me feel faint from the lowered calorie intake. I did not anticipate my body becoming ‘a technology of spirit’, turning into the female mediums I have been researching.2020 See further Sconce (2000) and Warner (2006). In mediumistic art, spirits can control the hands of artists and produce paintings. Hilma af Klint, for instance, built her practice through automatic writing and drawing in séances in Sweden, eventually producing 193 works in The Paintings for the Temple (1906-1915) cycle, allegedly commissioned by ‘High Masters’ she had been channeling. If I have already been immersed in documented séance communication across cultures in my research, why is it still so disconcerting to experience my own body in mediumship?

It was humbling, strange, but also invigorating to discover a side of me that has remained untapped. I was confronted by what Anzaldúa called “a tolerance of ambiguity” (79), a space of unknowing and not knowing, a space for divergent thinking that moves beyond known boundaries. It made me question whether I, too, as a researcher have fallen into the trap of the colonial anthropologist, interested in the exotic worlds of mediumship and trance and animist cosmologies, but never quite stepping into these communities in reciprocal relation. Are those of us working in the realm of the esoteric extracting from these practices, and what are the ethics of engagement with such practices, when they are ruled by an unseen realm?

About 9 months later, in an HTS expert meeting at V2_, I was explicitly asked to channel messages from beyond again. We all performed scrying under the guidance of Ginevra Petrozzi, who brought with her a book, Specchi Magici: Divination, evocation deli spiriti (2021), by Louis-Alphonse Cahagnet. Cahagnet was a famous French spiritualist and occultist who carried out research on magical magnetism. This Italian book is translated and excerpted from Magie Magnetique (1858), and includes a specific focus on magical mirrors. I have never heard of the term ‘scrying’ before, but I knew of this divination technique that has been found cross-culturally in different parts of the world. From crystal balls to bronze mirrors to water reflections, practitioners would look into reflective surfaces to find answers to their queries. We were invited to find a comfortable corner in the V2_ office, and we had to stare into the black mirrors of our screens until we had visions. We were told to look intensely without staring. Afterwards we discussed what we saw, or rather, what danced in the periphery of our visions and in our minds. Sceptical but curious, all of us spread across the space to scry for 30 minutes.

While some of us preferred darkness, I was drawn to natural light. I turned off my iPhone screen, and sat cross-legged on a wide window ledge overlooking the streets of Rotterdam. I turned on Solfeggio sound healing music2121 Solfeggio frequencies are specific sound tones that form a specific vibrational scale that are tonic tools believed to awaken higher consciousness, balance, and bring healing, as they resonate with the human body and its chakras in specific ways. and isolated my hearing senses from the room for better concentration. I tilted the angle of my screen slowly but constantly, searching for the most interesting reflection. I noticed the cracks on my protective screen, and traced them mindlessly with my eyes. Upside down in this reflection, I saw buildings and rooftops, graffiti and plants, muted and darkened in colours, but nothing was out of the ordinary. Boredom rose within me, as well as doubt as the clock ticked on—but as a seasoned meditation practitioner, I observed those feelings and let them float away. I emptied my mind of thoughts, grounded my body, and reminded myself that my alleged clairvoyant talents might come in handy in this exercise. Perhaps, I would successfully mediate something from beyond this realm?

I brought the black mirror closer to my face, and I studied my eyes, my eyebrows, and my forehead. I have never looked at my own reflection this way. The limited scope reminded me of the tiny mirrors that often accompany makeup-kits, just enough for someone to add a touch of eye-shadow or a lip colour. It was thoroughly defamiliarising my own face.

And this was when distortions to my reflection slowly unravelled.

As if in a time-lapse video, the skin around my eyes began to shrivel and form wrinkles. Lines formed between my eyebrows, as if I carried the frustrations of the world. Disturbed by this image, I relaxed my eyebrows consciously but my reflection remained the same. I was knotting them furiously. I began to age uncontrollably in front of my very own eyes. I felt slight tinges of fear and revulsion. And just as I became an old hex, I saw flashes of myself in youthfulness. Scenes of my eyes now under heavy theatrical makeup played. Colourful, sparkly, and glittery, it resembled the kind of makeup I used to do when I had stage performances. Shuttling between old age and youthfulness, I watched a montage of myself jumping between different ages.

Time was compressed into the now.

Nothing was linear.

All was an illusion.

Later, we would all come back into the room spilling stories of our visions. Some saw apocalyptic images, others saw dreams within dreams. Some came with reflections on the inhumane rationality of technology, and others interpreted their unconscious through Tarot card imagery. I was surprised by the richness of the experience — we paid no attention to what was ‘real’ and what was ‘acceptable’ in discourse; we simply held space for what came up, whether through cognitive thoughts, visions, or associations. We jokingly said that we all should probably stare more often at the black mirrors of our smartphones, scrying instead of scrolling, as resistance to the attention economy of capture so pertinent to our devices.

Towards a Holistic Method

No one knows what a body can do.

Through channelling and embodied research, I discovered extra layers to myself in the process of exploring a holistic method. When writing this essay, I questioned whether I would like to ‘come out’ in public through narrating these spiritual experiences which would no doubt challenge my scientific integrity as a researcher at a well-regarded university. I asked myself whether I was willing to take the risk of being discounted and ridiculed: Has she gone native? Has she gone off the deep end? How is any of this scientific? How is any of this publishable?

There is a certain queerness attached to esoteric research, in the sense that admitting to a scientific audience that I work on esoteric topics often feels like ‘coming out’. Queerness appears here as a rejection of ‘normality’ and brings to light what belongs to the margins. It also means to insist on particular forms of subjectivity, and to question what it means to live in a different body, in this case, a psychic, clairvoyant one. As much as I would like to hide these queer, non-conforming parts of myself at work, the need to be whole and to be wholly authentic trumped my concerns. I have to come to scholarship as my fully embodied and spiritual self, to mend the many cuts I have had to endure in order to be legible and recognised in the European academy. It is bell hooks’s sage wisdom in “Theory as Liberatory Practice” (1991) that rings in my ear—

I came to theory because I was hurting—the pain within me was so intense that I could not go on living. I came to theory desperate, wanting to comprehend—to grasp what was happening around and within me. Most importantly, I wanted to make the hurt go away. I saw in theory a location for healing.(1)

These words have long been my anchor. Like hooks who wishes to understand her African-American experience of double oppression as black and female in the US, I have come to theory to narrate my own negotiations of mixed ancestry, gender identity, and subjecthood, growing up in a postcolony between the East and the West. Yet European theory did not offer as much a space of refuge as it created boxes and pigeonholes which I had to contort my body to fit into. And this body of mine, literal and metaphorical, no longer wishes to perform such gymnastics. I have to author different methods and theories, such that theorisations of my somatic life could open up space for others to transform their epistemic practices in ways that could address their lived experiences too. How might we heal the cuts created by hierarchies of mind over body and spirit in our collective lifeworld? How do we relearn the somatic wisdom carried by witches, or the spiritual wisdom practiced by many non-Western formerly-colonised cultures?

Through channeling, I entered into an epistemic space that had no reference, at least not in scientific books. I embraced “radical vulnerability”, a “politics of indeterminacy, or a politics without guarantees” (Nagar 13, as quoted in McKittrick 17). I sought to understand spiritual technologies that have been passed down in my own ancestral cultures. I realised that a Teacher could be an immaterial one that could only be felt, and never seen. As McKittrick writes, “the unhinging, unknowing ourselves, opens up learning processes that are uninterested in a self that is economized by citations” (16). My desire to push the boundaries of my scholarship into the holistic was answered by unorthodox experiences, where no citations exist. In these experiences, my tolerance for ambiguity grew. I have more bandwidth now for the strange, the uncanny, and the ineffable. In fact, this is one logical conclusion to my commitment to the spirit of decolonisation, to refuse using the term as metaphor (Tuck and Yang). I have to fully accept the consequences of my own curiosity — the female body in mediumship, the Chinese ancestral body in trance, and the edges of my consciousness reflected back to me in the black mirror.

In accepting these direct experiences as part of my lifeworld, I integrate the parts of myself that I have had to cast out in the name of science. Perhaps, in this sense, the holistic is also an ethic of showing up in authenticity—to be whole again.

In search of the holistic, I plunged into unknown worlds and re-emerged, transformed and transfigured in the process—to affect and be affected, to feel and be felt, to think and be held in thoughts, to enter into trance, to become a medium between worlds. This radical auto-ethnographic account is not trying to convince you that these parallel worlds exist — I already know they do, but the methods of European science will not transport you there. Following McKittrick’s argument in Dear Science and Other Stories, methodological and theoretical frameworks always already pre-determine the shapes of conclusions—a rational framework will explain away the irrational and the esoteric as fancies of the mind. McKittrick goes on to suggest that “radical theory-making takes place outside existing systems of knowledge” (23)—so, how might we escape these pre-determinations and venture outside?

To enter these parallel pluriversal worlds, I suggest you take a deep breath and take a detour via the holistic and the somatic. Attend to your body and spirit in addition to your mind. Return to the body and to direct experience. And this detour can take place via artistic modalities of engagement that aim to extend the capacities of our body, retune our senses, and generate such direct experiences.

The Holistic Technology Salon uses art as a mode of inquiry that invites and enables each of us to ‘research’ our own bodies and our senses. By placing emphasis on intervention through experiential means, the collective holds space for pathways of discovery, bringing voice to the parts of us which have been numbed, muted, and silenced in the capitalist-inflected, rationality-centred lifeworld.

In this sense, a holistic method, whether in arts or sciences, emerges as an open-ended exploration. It is not universalising as a gesture, and does not attempt to force specific ontological and cosmological visions of different worlds onto this one. Instead, it remains attentive to our bodies and the various cosmovisions they inhabit, in the recognition that there are multiple portals and doorways into the many ‘pluriverses’around us, within and beyond Europe. Like holistic medicine that is often tailored to individual bodily constitution, a holistic method is also about the specificity of each individual body and how one relates differently to oneself, to one’s lineage, to each other, to technologies of the spirit and otherwise, and to the cosmos at large.

A holistic method is not a hypothetical. It is to be lived.

As part of the Holistic Technology Salon, I sincerely invite you to join us on this journey of reclaiming our bodies, our hearts, our spirits, and our souls from the grips of capitalist, patriarchal modernity. Together, we insist on the flourishing of other worlds in parallel to and beyond this one.

A Toolkit for Deep Planetary Sensing

Human perception extends beyond the five familiar senses, as other species demonstrate through their ability to detect electrical fields, infrared radiation, or magnetism. Intuition and ultrasensorial perception invite us to question the boundaries of what we consider knowable.

What is a sense you rarely use? What is your favourite sense? How do you sense things that excite you? How does your body respond in certain environments, situations, during specific activities. Here an overview of the manifold modalities of sense making available to humans.

1. Expanding the senses:

The five traditionally recognised human senses are:

  1. Sight (vision) — perceiving light, colour, shape, movement, and space through the eyes.
  2. Hearing (audition) — perceiving sound, frequency, rhythm, and vibration through the ears.
  3. Smell (olfaction) — detecting airborne chemical compounds through the nose.
  4. Taste (gustation) — perceiving flavours such as sweet, sour, salty, bitter, and umami through receptors primarily on the tongue.
  5. Touch (tactition) — sensing pressure, texture, vibration, temperature, and other stimuli through receptors in the skin.

Beyond the five senses

From a scientific perspective, humans have many additional sensory systems, including:

  • Proprioception — awareness of the position and movement of our bodies.
  • Interoception — sensing internal bodily states, such as hunger, thirst, heartbeat, and breathing.
  • Vestibular sense — perceiving balance, spatial orientation, and acceleration.
  • Nociception — detecting potentially damaging stimuli associated with pain.
  • Thermoception — sensing temperature.
  • Chronoception — our subjective perception of time, although it is not generally classified as a single, discrete sense.

The sixth sense

When including the ultrasensorial our systems have very different ways of communicating with us, some people ‘sense’ information with their whole body / being, others smell pleasant or repulsive scents, others see….

  • Clairvoyance — “clear seeing”; receiving information through inner images, visions, or visual impressions.
  • Clairaudience — “clear hearing”; receiving information as inner voices, words, sounds, or music.
  • Clairsentience — “clear feeling”; sensing information through bodily sensations, emotions, or subtle impressions.
  • Claircognizance — “clear knowing”; experiencing a sudden, seemingly intuitive knowing without an identifiable reasoning process.
  • Clairalience — “clear smelling”; experiencing smells without an identifiable external source.
  • Clairgustance — “clear tasting”; experiencing tastes without an identifiable external source.
  • Clairtangency — “clear touching,” sometimes associated with psychometry; purportedly receiving information through touch or contact with objects.
  • Clair-empathy — sensing or intuitively experiencing the emotions of others, sometimes considered a form of clairsentience.
  • Intuition — arriving at an impression or understanding without consciously tracing the reasoning behind it.
  • Telepathy — transmission or reception of thoughts between minds without conventional sensory communication.
  • Precognition — perception of events before they occur.
  • Retrocognition — perception of past events beyond ordinary means of knowing.
  • Remote viewing — perceive information about distant or hidden locations or targets without ordinary sensory access.

2. Inner Imprints: Our experiences, memories, and cultural conditioning shape the filters through which we perceive and interpret reality. In Vedic philosophy for example, samskaras describe impressions that influence habitual patterns of perception and response.

How might recognising these imprints allow us to question automatic patterns and open ourselves to new experiences? What are your filters? What does reality look like through the eyes of your neighbour, your pet, a tree?

3. Identity: Our identities are shaped by the interplay of bodily, emotional, cultural, ancestral, and relational dimensions. These frameworks influence how we understand ourselves and what we consider possible.

What might change if we understood identity as relational and continuously evolving rather than fixed? How does your identity feel? Is it heavy or light, does it change throughout the day?

4. Breath: Breath connects physiological processes with conscious awareness, influencing attention, nervous-system states, and our experience of time.

How does our perception change when we return our attention to the present moment? Breath is also an amazing way to hack time;slow down your breath and time expands…

5. Rebalance Activity and Receptivity: Western culture privileges productivity, control, and measurable output, often at the expense of receptivity and contemplation.

Practice receiving information consciously, this can be any sensory modality, listening, smelling, seeing, being touched by the air around you. Let the information come to you. How does it feel to be present rather than trying to act upon the world?

6. AND/AND States: Symbolic frameworks such as yin and yang allow us to explore the interplay of qualities often coded as masculine and feminine, including analysis and intuition, action and receptivity. Rather than privileging one over the other, we can cultivate their complementarity.

Allow your mind and emotions and sensations to be present, even though they might seem contradictory. Invite them all to tell you what they want without the need for a specific immediate outcome. Continue with your day to day activities in your regular modus operandi and come back to this exercise when you feel called to. It is a gradual attunement.

7. Myths: Myths, archetypes, and cultural narratives shape our understanding of identity, power, and what futures we can imagine. Examining these stories can reveal how fear, control, and domination become normalised while alternative ways of relating remain marginalised.

What are your favourite myths and characters? What are their values and attributes? What would an archetype look like that embodies values of care and relationality while being cool and grounded? What is the opposite archetype? Your imagination is limitless…

Ultimately it is about recognizing when we act from fear, which leads to contraction and control, and when we act from love, trust and care. This requires a lot of patience… and LOVE.

Through multidisciplinary collaborations, I examine how, throughout history, technologies have transformed human modes of seeing, sensing, and relating—and how collective shifts in consciousness, in turn, give rise to new technological forms and scientific paradigms. My practice unravels this exchange by investigating its effects on self-image, intersubjectivity, visual culture, and societal structures.

Working across painting, print, scent, sound, performance, and multisensory installations, I foreground sensory and ultrasensorial exploration as a mode of knowledge production. Indebted to Pauline Oliveros, my projects seek to cultivate receptivity and intuitive awareness as complements to the dominant rationalist frameworks governing technoscientific cultures. Influenced by posthumanist thinkers such as Rosi Braidotti and Karen Barad, I question binary oppositions such as mind versus body or nature versus culture, proposing instead a monistic worldview that acknowledges the agency of non-human beings and materials. Within this expanded ontology, humans are not separate observers of the Earth but participants in a continuously co-created, multispecies world. My work invites audiences to sense these interdependencies directly.

Deep Planetary Sensing seeks to contribute to a positive expansion of our human ability to make sense of the world and live in the right relation with the global family of minerals, plants, animals, and humans. It proposes a paradigm shift: from disembodied productivity towards embodied relationality, from fragmented perception towards ecological attunement, and from fear-driven culture towards one grounded in collective interconnection. Through a multisensory, interdisciplinary approach, my work seeks to expand the boundaries of perception and challenge dominant epistemologies, guiding us towards a more integrated experience of self, technology, and planet—one in which consciousness, matter, and life are understood as interdependent.

The future is useful only when not tormented

Ginevra Petrozzi

The need to predict the future in advance has historically been used to do damage control. Predictions were made to answer specific or less specific questions, and after being received they entered the world, based on how the receiver would activate them. In literature, we have famous examples of decisions being made after a future prediction had been made and things subsequently going terribly wrong. In the case of Oedipus Rex, narrated by Sophocles, the protagonist famously tries to escape his future as it is predicted and delivered as fate. The moral of the whole story is that one’s fate cannot be escaped no matter what one does. Actions might be taken to avoid it, but one will still face it. Oedipus lays with his mother and kills his father, as predicted, not despite his efforts to escape his fate, but because of them (“I have suffered, friends, the worst horrors on earth, suffered against my will I swear to god, not a single thing self-willed—”)11 Sophocles. (1984). Oedipus Rex (R. Fagles, Trans.). Penguin Classics. (Original work published ca. 429 BCE). In fact, we will never know how the story would have unfolded if Oedipus did not try to escape his fate with all his strength, and instead accepted it, or perhaps questioned it.

The way predictions seem to enter the realm of decision making often appears to be at different times. For Oedipus, fate is delivered as a certainty. Decisions can be made around those certainties, but not directly about them. In another notable example, narrated by Herodotus, Croesus, the king of Lydia asked the Oracle if he should pursue a war with Persia. Pythia answered: “If you go to war against Persia, you will destroy a great empire”2.2 Herodotus, A. D. Godley, Herodotus, and Herodotus. Herodotus. The Loeb Classical Library 117–120. Harvard University Press, 1990), 1981. The answers of Pythia were famously delivered in a consciously ambiguous way and always implied the need for an interpretation. Pythia, because of the powers she was invested in by the gods, could only utter the truth, so her predictions were also received as truths. In her case, though, the final interpretations were left to the querant, who had to decide how the predictions would become actionable. The king of Lydia, presented with his fate, interprets it and decides to move forward with his bellic endeavors, and perhaps too late, finds out that the great empire that will find its sunset is his own.

In early China, although upper-level military decisions were made by the state, battlefield divination protocols were frequently repeated to determine and verify the most auspicious dates for military action.33 Yates, Robin D. S. “The History of Military Divination in China.” East Asian Science, Technology, and Medicine, no. 24 (2005): 15–43. Both cases highlight the interplay of prediction, interpretation, and decision-making. In these stories, it is taken for granted that the future is written regardless of the actions being taken towards or against it. Reading the future in advance eliminates doubt about what will surely happen. Divination practices have been described as epistemic technologies, used to “obtain factually accurate information about the world in order to inform decisions and actions”4,4 Hong, Ze, and Joseph Henrich. “The Cultural Evolution of Epistemic Practices.” Human Nature 32, no. 3 (2021): 622–51. https://doi.org/10.1007/s12110-021-09408-6. and therefore accuracy was deemed important. Divination practices have helped humans evolve ‘epistemic vigilance’, mostly to ensure that diviners were not charlatans, tricksters or incompetent.

For contemporary readers, these practices appear striking and do not fit with the causal understanding of reality. Currently, the world is understood as a probabilistic machine in which each individual choice can build the future brick by brick. The sociologist Esposito has extensively written about this concept, which she refers to as “Open Future”. The Open Future allows decisions to be disentangled from a pre-written future, allowing the concept of free-will. Based on this idea, the future does not exist; therefore, any attempt to predict it (seeing it in advance) is bound to fail. Regardless, Esposito argues, divinatory attitudes linger even within an Open Future, and algorithmic processes replicate in many ways the arbitrariness of fortune telling. As the physicist Vespignani writes “Algorithms are close to fulfilling the desire for a secret-free, completely predictable tomorrow”55 Vespignani, Alessandro, and Rosita Rijtano. L’algoritmo e l’oracolo: come la scienza predice il futuro e ci aiuta a cambiarlo. Il saggiatore, 2019. , an idea which cannot truly take place in the ontological description of an Open Future. Esposito notes that algorithms do not fit in the modern semantics of our current future; rather, they are much more in line with the attitude of divination.

Whatever method is being used to retrieve information about the future, efficacy cannot be the focal point. Predictions, both ancient and modern, should not be expected to successfully predict the future in advance. Still, it is a practice that has endured through time and that still proves useful because humans always strive to know more about what is unknown. “In today’s anthropological literature on divination, there seems to be an implicit assumption that because divination is not objectively effective, it cannot be the case that people are striving to obtain more accurate information about the world. This narrative logic ignores the fact that it is ultimately subjectively perceived efficacy that matters, and objective efficacy is not the only factor that contributes to subjective perception"6.6 Hong & Henrich, 2021

The focal point of predictive methods should be usefulness, not objective efficacy. “Common alternative explanations have proposed that divination is a way to (1) claim political legitimacy and power, (2) reduce anxiety, (3) circumvent indecision and resolve disputes, or express particular intuitions”7.7 Hong & Henrich, 2021 This is explained by Hong and Henrick, suggesting that none of these uses for divination are necessarily meant to know the future in advance. Divinatory predictions are always useful no matter how correct they are, because they produce an image of the future to negotiate with.

Our current experience with predictive systems, although seemingly very far from Oedipus' fate, resembles in many ways the experience of humans living in ancient societies and using divinatory practices. AI, similarly to divination, fulfills a desire to eliminate doubt, reduce anxiety about the future, and give agency to users, which are all characteristics one could identify in divinatory practices as well. As is, we seemingly do not doubt the reliability of AI as much as we may doubt the reliability of divinatory practices. Yet, in their structure, they are fundamentally equal: they are trying to deal with uncertainty whilst being inextricably uncertain in and of themselves.

The future is now open, predictions are based on real data, and the world is understood numerically. Yet, uncertainty persists, and the human condition seems to have barely shifted: “In ancient times the future appeared to be unknowable to humans, but not to divinity, not to God. As for today, the future appears to be unknowable to humans but not to algorithms”8,8 Esposito, Elena. “The Structures of Uncertainty: Performativity and Unpredictability in Economic Operations.” Economy and Society 42, no. 1 (2013): 102–29. https://doi.org/10.1080/03085147.2012.687908. as Esposito argues.

In India, Vedic astrology is considered an “astral science” rather than a religious practice, although it is mostly performed within Hinduist religious belief circles. Vedic astrology is incredibly precise and based on scientific mathematical calculations of the position of planets and stars at specific times and places. With geolocation and precise time, it is possible to calculate the astral chart, also called Kundali, of virtually anything. The birth of someone, the launch of a rocket, the start of a company.

This first astrologer gave me a two-hour long consultation that was extremely precise and generously detailed. I was told I could get the best possible outcome for myself by following specific rituals and reciting prayers. I was determined to have the best version of my future I could and I started to recite the mantras given to me once I arrived in India. I had to recite one sentence 108 times a day for 40 days. By day 3 I had forgotten, and felt the weight of my destiny falling on me. My ‘Oedipus’ sentence. Was I locked with that future forever? If I couldn’t even keep up with simple prayers, how could I confront the worst outcomes out of all that had been predicted? According to the first astrologer, I was not going to get married, and I would probably want children, but it would be too late for me to have them. Stuck with these ‘truths’, and without enough willpower to pray for better, I decided to escape this one totalizing future.

I consulted a different astrologer. Next to my residency space there was a local Jyotisha and after passing by in front of his shop a few times, I made up my mind and went in. The consultation was careful and shorter than the first one, but more importantly –different. Some aspects were similar to the first one, but major aspects were almost the opposite. According to him I should have gotten married before June 2025! I spent three weeks in Bangalore and I consulted another astrologer, then a palm reader, then a face reader. Both palm reading and face reading use the time and place of birth of the querent to interpret their future. So, even though on paper they are different branches of Vedic astrology with different nuances, they are still informed by the same astral chart.

All the consultations were different; some gave very precise dates when an event would happen, while some were more generic and open ended. All of them touched upon what one might define as “matters of life”., Love, health, professional career, death and many more. From all the consultations I now had many options of how things would go for me. Dates and times, nuances, threats and blessings. None of them mattered anymore, or felt as heavy as the sentence Oedipus received. They suddenly became possible futures. All of them potentially true and plausible. I may get married, or not. I may have children when it is too late, or perhaps at the perfect time. I may have success in my career abroad, or perhaps I may decide to settle in one place and stay until the end of my days. As for Oedipus, it is not about what predictions we might receive, but how we are going to act on them.

I think of the need to predict the future as very human, almost endearing. We seek certainty to make sure that we will make the right decisions. Or, that regardless of how dark reality looks in the present, something better will come. Somehow, after these five consultations, new possible paths opened in front of me. Many Lives I could live. In Vedic tradition it is not possible to change one’s future entirely. But it is possible to get the best possible outcome, within the fixed terms arranged by the stars. In a joking way, I asked one of the astrologers, specialized in face reading, if I might be able to change my future by changing a facial feature, like undergoing a nose-job or re-arranging my teeth into straighter positions. He laughed and said that no, of course that wasn't possible. I laughed with him, thinking of a project of mine I had developed some years ago called “Bite Off More Than You Can Chew”.

Face reading is a widespread divination practice, one that reads facial features to know specific aspects of one’s temperament, personality, but also future. During the time in which I was wearing braces, and entirely changing my teeth placement and disposition, I asked myself if I might be able to change my future too. I underwent a very complex process, together with creative programmer Matus Solcany, to calculate my future based on the movement of my teeth. My dentists provided me with specific measurements for movement in my mouth through the two years period of wearing the braces, and I researched the meaning each tooth had in an interpolation of Holistic medicine, Indian face reading and Chinese teeth-medicine.

Starting the process at week 23 of my orthodontic treatment, I was in the privileged position to be able to ‘train’ a data set based on those past weeks, and use it to calculate the remaining 21 weeks of life. In my contorted reasoning, if indeed I was changing my future with the movement of my teeth, I could correlate what was happening in my life with the directions the teeth were taking. Based on that correlation, I could then have known the unknown x to the equation, in which x was my future.

Past events : Past teeth movement = x : Future Teeth Movement

And so I did it. With the precious help of Matus, I labeled and graded the first 23 weeks of treatment based on 103 categories. These would help me to evaluate my life in all possible ways. Spanning from health, love, spirituality, work, the well being of specific organs, good news, bad news…The procedure was strenuous. In order to grade my past, I needed to go through my digital and mnemonic archive: what was I doing four months ago? When did I get ill? When did I receive a work opportunity? When did I gain money or lose it? I was serious.

After having evaluated on a scale from 1 to 10 the 23 weeks of life, week per week (that’s how often my teeth would take different turns), the numerical values were correlated to the movement of the teeth my dentists had provided me. Based on the future teeth movement, we then were able to generate new numerical values, or a ‘grading’ of the future weeks. With a bit of imagination and help from a very primordial GPT-3, those grades became sentences that would inform me of what would go well, great or terribly in my upcoming future.

I won’t go into detail here on what my teeth told me. Partly, because it’s no one's business, and no one would find it useful except me. Partly, I asked Matus to keep the file himself and not show it to me until the end of the 21 weeks. The end of my future. Why did I do all this if I refused to then read what my future (allegedly) held?I find that through these projects, both my teeth and my stars were suggesting that I not torment myself about the future. There is no definite solution to the uncertainty that we carry, imprinted in our DNA as human beings, only narrations we might like more or less.I hold all these contradictions close to my heart, to my guts. What I enjoy the most is trying out every possible and available method to take a peek into any version of the future available. Not because I wish it to be true, or because I want to believe in something, but because in those versions of the future,I live more lives than I ever could without knowing And who knows, if I ever get old, I might even have the chance to say: I knew it all along.

What does it mean to be in 'right relationship'?

Sieta van Horck

I would like to start with gratitude: thank you Mother Earth for all our relatives and relationships, our human and non-human kin.

The wisdom of burn-out

Our current Western culture is built on the concept of individualism; the old Darwinian idea that we have to compete to survive, that your existence is a threat to mine. Humans are dominated by this myth of separation. We are hypnotized, obsessively staring at ourselves through black mirrors, trying to understand and heal ourselves so we can live our best lives. Be the best version of yourself. Maximize your output, manifest your dreams, speed up, consume more, manifest more. We have fallen into the abyss of neo-liberal capitalism. Even though it has been sold to us in a shiny wellness jacket. When wellness is divorced from land, ceremony, community, and context, it becomes consumption.

The same mechanism that is colonizing the land, is colonizing our thoughts. Hand in hand with technological progress, we are led astray by our own minds, fetishising productivity. Internalized capitalism. We speed up, consume more information, more connections, more data, only to find our minds are tired and our bodies beat down by the pressure of trying to uphold whatever ideology the hyper-individualized hypnotic gaze is prescribing us to. What if the same extractive systems that push ecosystems to collapse are also burning us out? The planet is heating up and so are we. Our bodies evolved alongside the natural world, guided by sunrise, sunset and the steady cycles of seasons and circadian rhythms. But capitalism demands urgency, speed and constant output. What happens when our biology– designed for rhythm and renewal– is forced to operate in systems that never slow down? The nervous system wasn’t meant to thrive under capitalism. We’re expected to hustle through exhaustion, celebrate burnout and treat rest as laziness. Our value is tied to our productivity, where our bodies are viewed as machines meant for endless output. Research on job burnout shows that chronic workplace stress and burnout are associated with a range of physical, psychological and occupational consequences, highlighting the importance of prevention and early intervention (Salvagioni et al., 2017). Our nervous systems were built for cycles of stress and rest/recovery. Not endless urgency.

What is the climate crisis? Is it also a nervous system crisis? What if we are not only extracting resources from the Earth, but from every living being — including ourselves? If the Earth is dysregulated, how can we expect our bodies to remain well? Ecological grief, climate anxiety and burnout can be understood as responses to the same rupture: our growing disconnection from balance, belonging and the living world. Ecological grief, in particular, has been described as a legitimate emotional response to ecological and climate-related loss (Cunsolo & Ellis, 2018).

How can we make space for these emotional responses, rather than suppressing or individualising them? Together with Rens Spanjaard and Extinction Rebellion, I co-founded Green Rebels, a collective exploring grief, regeneration and ecological connection as forms of social and political practice. Our work is inspired by Joanna Macy’s Work That Reconnects, and particularly its Spiral: a cyclical process moving through the stages of Coming from Gratitude, Honoring Our Pain for the World, Seeing with New/Ancient Eyes, and Going Forth (Macy & Brown, 2014). Rather than seeing grief, anger or despair as emotions that need to be overcome, the Work That Reconnects creates space to acknowledge and honour these responses to the state of the world and to transform them into connection, renewed perspective and action. Through Green Rebels, we bring this process into public space through collective rituals. These regenerative transformation rituals create moments to acknowledge ecological loss, move through grief together, and open up new ways of relating to the world around us. They invite a shift in perspective: from seeing nature as something outside of us, to understanding the living world as a teacher, a relative, and a model for how we might live.

The Earth teaches us cyclicality. Bears hibernate, trees shed leaves, tides retreat. No single being blooms all year. Regulation is natural and essential. What if we learned from Earth's intelligence? What if humans step out of the story of separation and instead, adopt the story of interbeing? What if we learned from Earth’s intelligence? Healing is not just individual–it's collective. Systems that care for bodies like ecosystems are possible. What if we built societies that allowed us, like nature, to pause, repair and thrive in rhythm? The individual is a large body that extends beyond boundaries. We have to stop navel gazing–obsessively focusing on our own thoughts, data, feelings, problems. We have to start seeing ourselves through the lens of relationality. Lean in to us, instead of me. If it’s not relational, it’s not healing.

Right Relationship

‘The Sense of Self is porous – it is not limited to our skin, it is territory,’ writes Dr Bayo Akomolafe. The Self is not a fixed point but a living territory, one that flows beyond the skin, intertwining with the land, the stories, and the communities that shape us. This perspective challenges modern ideas of individuality, reminding us that who we are is porous and deeply entwined with the world around us (Akomolafe, 2023). Just as rivers carve the land and treeroots intertwine beneath our soil, our sense of self pulses through the relationships we nurture, the places we inhabit and the ancestral memory we carry. We have to start getting into right relationship. We weren’t meant to live this fast. Our bodies push back with anxiety, exhaustion, and illness. We were made for slowness, connection, care, nature, laughter and deep presence. That is where we belong. And that’s where we can return to. So how can we return, or better yet, remember? Technology is ubiquitous and forever speeding things up. Is it possible to live alongside technology, to have a meaningful relationship with it - or should we abandon it all together and live a secluded life in the mountains, herding sheep and listening to the wind blowing through the trees? Can we relate to the technosphere as an integral part of the planetary experience from a care-centric perspective?

Technology is –as is everything else that exists in this universe, a part of the web of life. However, taking inspiration from Tyson Yunkaporta and his Indigenous thinking as described in his book Sand Talk–there can be a wrong relationship and a right relationship to these technologies (Yunkaporta, 2020). Right relationship depends on our human intention of how we use it. If we use technology while being in ‘wrong relationship’ – meaning not in line with the natural rhythms of mother Earth – contemporary life diverges from the pattern of creation.Right relationship is something that lives deeply in our cells. It is something that is known, for no (obvious) reason. It is not something we have to look for, rather it is something that emerges as a knowing. Our flesh is part of the Earth, and so our cells remember how. For this to emerge though, we need stillness.

Our black mirror not only reflects our own distorted images back to us; it also captures and harvests our attention. With our minds in a constant state of arousal and distraction, it can become difficult to be still enough for a deeper kind of knowing to emerge. The contemporary attention economy treats attention as a resource to be captured and extracted, while Jenny Odell invites us to reconsider attention as a practice of reconnecting with ourselves, our surroundings and the more-than-human world (Odell, 2019).

I wanted to explore this relationship with our digital devices in a more direct and embodied way. Together with SETUP, I created Techyoga: a yoga class in which the asanas are designed specifically for you and your phone. By bringing the device into the practice rather than asking participants to simply put it away, the workshop invites us to pause and contemplate our relationship with the technology that has become so intimately entangled with our everyday lives. Alongside the workshop, we created a small book featuring eleven yoga asanas that can be practised independently, offering playful ways to reflect on (and perhaps reconsider) our relationship with our devices.

Rethinking, remembering, repairing

However, what was deliberately built to catch eye-balls to feed the capitalist machine, can also be developed with a sense of care and community built into it. What would our technology look like if we were to be motivated by values other than monetary wealth? Can we think of AI as an archive for Indigenous information that otherwise might get lost, such as with the project Tainá is doing in Brazil? Can we rethink AI in research with ancestral wisdom for future generations? Can AI foster justice through democratic and diplomatic governance, the sharing of knowledge, the protection of diversity, and service to humanity and the planet? What would community ownership for AI look like? We need to start learning how we can be in a right relationship with our humanity and subsequently with technology. How we do this is by remembering that we are not separate from the systems we inhabit. Not from the forests, not from the waters, not from the technologies we create, and not from one another.

As Charles Eisenstein writes, the crisis of our time is, at its core, a crisis of separation. A forgetting. We have inherited a story in which humans stand apart from nature, apart from community, apart even from our own bodies. We have learned to see the world as a collection of inert resources rather than living relations. The forest becomes timber. The river becomes utility. The body becomes a machine. Attention becomes currency. Time becomes a commodity. But the Earth does not organize herself this way (Eisenstein, 2013). The forest is not a collection of individual trees competing for sunlight. Beneath the soil, roots and fungi exchange nutrients in intricate systems of mutual aid. Trees communicate. They warn each other of danger. They share resources with the weak. The health of one depends on the health of all. This is not a metaphor. This is ecology. This is reality. And perhaps this is also an instruction. What if human flourishing was not based on optimization, but on reciprocity? What if success was measured by the quality of our relationships? What if wellness meant not simply feeling good, but being in a good healthy relationship with all that is? Robin Wall Kimmerer’s work on reciprocity and kinship with the more-than-human world offers another way of understanding flourishing: not as individual accumulation, but as participation in relationships of mutual exchange and responsibility (Kimmerer, 2013).

Vandana Shiva reminds us that biodiversity is not merely an environmental concern, it is a way of understanding life itself. Diversity creates resilience. Monocultures collapse. This is true in our agriculture, and it is true in our minds. Yet the dominant systems around us push toward sameness: standardized identities, standardized productivity, standardized desires. Algorithms flatten complexity. Capitalism rewards efficiency over intimacy (Shiva, 1993).

And still, life resists to be controlled: in the weeds growing through concrete, in the body asking for rest, in the grief that surfaces when we witness ecological loss, and in the longing for something slower, older, more true. These are not inconveniences to be overcome or distractions from the lives we are trying to build; they are signals, invitations to return. But return to what? Not to a romanticized past or to some imagined state of purity, nor to a life untouched by modernity, but to relationship, to the understanding that nothing exists independently, that every breath we take has already passed through forests and oceans, that the calcium in our bones was once carried through ancient seas, and that our nervous systems are shaped and regulated through presence, touch, connection and belonging. Loneliness, in this sense, is not simply an emotion we experience individually, but can also be understood as a biological and ecological alarm, a signal that something in our relationship to ourselves, to others, or to the world around us has been disrupted.

To come into right relationship is to move from ownership towards stewardship, from extraction towards reciprocity, and from control towards participation. It asks us to slow down enough to listen: to the body, to the land, to each other, and to the quiet intelligence that exists beneath thought. But how do we cultivate this sense of relationship in a world that so often teaches us to see ourselves as separate from the living systems that sustain us? How do we build practices that reawaken our sense of belonging to the vibrant web of life?

These questions formed the starting point for Feral Species and Ecosomatic Manifesto, a public space intervention and manifesto created during a residency at Krater in Ljubljana. The work explores the relationships of interbeing between multispecies bodies and local lands, bridging art, ecology and embodiment by interweaving Krater’s feral presence with the urban landscape of Ljubljana. Through carefully placed small reminders of interbeing throughout the cityscape, the work invites us to notice the relationships that are already there, but that dominant ways of living and seeing have taught us to overlook. The work emerged from the recognition that many of the crises of our time are rooted in a breakdown of the relationship between humans and the Earth, particularly within dominant Western cultures, where respect and reciprocity have increasingly been replaced by exploitation and extraction, and community by consumerism.

Rather than offering a solution to this rupture, Feral Species and Ecosomatic Manifesto asks us to practice noticing. It asks what might happen if we were to pay attention to the more-than-human worlds that surround us, to the plants growing through cracks in the pavement, to the soil beneath our feet, to the histories held within landscapes, and to the bodies through which we experience these places. In doing so, the work becomes an invitation to reconsider belonging not as something we possess, but as something we participate in, a living relationship that is continuously being made, unmade and remade.

Stillness is not passive; stillness is how we hear. And when we become still enough to listen, something ancient begins to surface, a cellular memory, a forgotten rhythm, a knowing that cannot be downloaded, quantified or optimized. Perhaps what we remember is not a specific way of living, but a way of being in relation: how to sit with grief without immediately rushing to solve it, how to gather and cook for one another, how to sing, how to watch the sky change colour, how to notice when the body says no, how to honour endings, and how to rest. Because rest, too, is relational.

When we rest, we interrupt the logic of endless production. We tell the body it is not merely valuable for what it can produce. We tell the Earth that we are willing to move at her pace. We reclaim time as something sacred rather than scarce. This may be one of the most radical acts available to us: to pause, to soften, to refuse urgency. Not because the world is not in crisis, but because it is. The dominant systems will not teach us how to heal from the harms they create. They will offer us solutions that often replicate the same extractive logic, green technologies built on mined suffering, wellness industries built on individual consumption, artificial intelligence trained on invisible labor and exhausted ecologies. And yet, technology itself is not the enemy.

Fire can warm or destroy. A knife can nourish or wound. Technology, too, carries no inherent morality. It amplifies the consciousness with which it is made and used. The question is not whether we reject technology. The question is whether we can bring ceremony into producing it, and using it. Can we approach our devices with intention rather than compulsion? Can we build digital spaces that nourish community rather than commodify attention? Can innovation emerge from humility rather than domination? What would it mean to design technology informed by ecological principles? Technology that values regeneration over growth, interdependence over competition, enoughness over expansion, care over convenience.

Making kin is a practice

Donna Haraway’s concept of “making kin” offers one way of thinking about these questions: not simply reproducing existing systems, but learning how to live and make-with others–human and non-human–within a damaged and interconnected world (Haraway, 2016). What would social media look like if it strengthened local communities? What could AI become if trained not only with data, but with ethical responsibility and ancestral wisdom? What would our digital architectures look like if they mirrored healthy ecosystems, diverse, adaptive, reciprocal and alive? Perhaps right relationship with technology begins exactly where right relationship with Earth begins: with reverence. With asking: What does this tool ask of my attention? What does it give? What does it take? Does it bring me closer to life? And perhaps the deeper question beneath all of this is not how do we fix the world, but how do we belong to it again? How do we become trustworthy participants in the web of life? The answer may be simpler than we imagine.

Touch the soil. Learn the names of the trees around you. Cook and eat in community. Let yourself grieve. Rest when your body asks. Notice the moon. Put the phone down. Listen for what emerges in the silence. The Earth is not asking us to save her. She is asking us to remember how to be her. To remember that we are not separate. That our bodies are made of borrowed elements. That our healing is bound together. If relationality is the medicine, then right relationship is the practice. Again and again, we return. To the breath. To the body. To the Earth beneath our feet. And perhaps this is where healing begins. Not in becoming something new, but in remembering what we already are.

Ancestral Healing in the Age of AI

Georgia Kareola

As I stepped into my friend’s apartment the other day, I noticed how his desktop screen displayed a conversation with a chatbot. A few days earlier, Jay had undergone surgery on his nose to correct a deviated septum. We jokingly called it “the nose job”. While spending the day recovering at home, Jay (a Millenial cusp Gen Z with a degree in engineering) had asked the chatbot whether the increasing pain he’d felt since the operation was normal.

Judging by the prominent place of the computer in the living room and the fact that it hadn’t been switched off in anticipation of my arrival, I concluded Jay wasn’t hiding this information from me. When he casually disappeared into the kitchen to prepare us some lunch, I, with a mixture of apprehension and curiosity, glanced over the dialogue. Chat had responded to Jay’s question in an empathic and comforting tone: the pain was, in fact, a good sign; a natural response of the body during the healing process. I felt relieved; a sign that my body acknowledged Chat’s words as a source of truth.

Although I was initially, admittedly, a little taken aback by the explicit presence of the mediated medical assistance in the room and momentarily intimidated by the intimate nature of the conversation, my adaptable human mind also, within seconds, accepted the chatbot’s presence as obvious. Of course, physical discomfort drove Jay to AI, not back to his doctor.

The data confirms the normalcy of Jay’s behavior. OpenAI reports that more than 40 million people worldwide ask ChatGPT questions about health every day, and that health‑related exchanges make up over 5% of all ChatGPT messages globally (which is a lot considering the amount of topics humans worldwide feed into the LLM). This amounts to billions of health‑related inquiries per week, which include questions about symptoms, medication, treatment options, and insurance coverage.

I will not spend much time on the undeniably valid criticisms of ChatGPT and OpenAI regarding its lack of data sovereignty, the founder’s financial support of the authoritarian US administration, and the company’s supply of ChatGPT-powered screening tools to ICE, nor on the current, widespread boycott of this bot. Instead, I will focus on what the cultural and (inter)personal implications are of people consulting AI and social media for health advice. I will do this by analysing three AI-generated, or synthetic, influencers, who purportedly speak from ancestral or ancient healing traditions. Through them, I will question what the ever-deepening penetration of AI, through social media or otherwise, into our lives means for our perception of, and relation with our bodies and these traditions. I will conclude with some reflections on how these technologies could be more holistic.

Health advice is disseminated all over the internet these days, and next to consulting LLMs like Claude and ChatGPT, people comb social media for advice on their conditions. A 2025 Edelman Trust Barometer survey of about 16,000 people across 16 countries found that younger adults (Gen Z and Millennials) increasingly favor health advice from friends, family, and social media over doctors, with 38% of Gen Z reportedly ignoring medical guidance in favor of social‑media content. To anecdotally validate this: over the past weeks I had several conversations with friends who, although classifying as Millenials, indicated that "doctors are useless" or unable to give tailored advice applicable to their conditions, and that they instead turn to AI or social media, as they tend to find more appropriate (or more soothing, that much wasn’t clear to me) answers to their questions there.

When it comes to social media specifically, a 2023 global healthcare‑information report found that 33% of Gen Z, 25% of Millennials, about 14% of Gen X, and 5% of baby boomers use social media to discuss illness, with TikTok the top platform for Gen Z, followed by Instagram and Twitter/X. The study also shows that 38% of respondents do not view their doctor as a ‘regular information source’.

There are obviously serious downsides to this trend, some of which The World Health Organization Europe has identified. In a recent report, they highlight a concern that misinformation on platforms like Twitter, Facebook, YouTube, and Instagram can distort health beliefs, increase fear, and worsen self‑care and treatment decisions, naming it a major public‑health concern. To remedy this, they call for new legal policies and plans to increase people’s digital and health literacy.”

Questions like “what’s true?” and “what’s trustworthy?” are ever more pressing and ever more difficult to answer in data-flooded online bubbles. When it comes to health specifically, there have long been multiple perspectives to choose from, from your GP’s to Ayurveda, to TCM. With the rise of the internet and online influencers, the perspectives available have expanded to include self-acclaimed yet socially validated health experts like the Raw Vegan Yoga mom on Bali and the LA High Fat Carnivore. If you lack the education or digital literacy to discern between fact-checked or health information and BS, or the ability (for whatever reason) to question whether other people’s health advice is actually healthy for you, you may end up being fed whatever appears on your feed.

With Western medical doctors losing ground and credibility, perhaps partially because of the time pressure they are under to see patients (in the Netherlands, a patient gets 10 minutes with a GP in a regular consultation), and partially because it is increasingly difficult for doctors to compete with the immediacy, abundance of information, and artificial empathy provided by AI and content creators, the road becomes paved to a medical wild west, gold diggers included.

Western medicine was never a holy grail. Critical social scientists, such as Samikshya Dhimal argue that modern biomedicine’s authoritarian, paternalistic logics originate in European colonialism and have been reconstituted and intensified under neoliberal capitalism, turning patients into consumers and commodifying well‑being. The rise of interest in alternative care providers is understandable, yet the danger is that people shift their focus and hope to less reliable and more exploitative sources.

Synthetic Ancestors

Wellness influencers became popular in the 2010s, and because of their often sizable following and the fact that wellness products are sold best by people who appear both fit and credible, companies like to collaborate with influencers to pitch products to their audiences. A more recent phenomenon is AI-generated, or synthetic, health influencers, who are brought into existence exclusively for marketing purposes, while disguised as ancestral healers.

@mamaabeniroots is an AI-generated African woman with, at its peak, 116K followers on Instagram, who, in her bio, claims to provide "Ancestral African healing for body and spirit". Dressed in wavy organic fabric and decorated with beaded necklaces, big earrings, and a patterned headscarf partially covering her dreadlocked hair, Mama Abeni shares health advice on a wide variety of topics. Because of the way she looks and speaks, with kindness and authority, it undeniably feels convincing. I admit, when I first encountered Mama Abeni, I thought she was real.

My suspicion rose when, upon closer inspection, I noticed the consistently sensational headlines, such as "Sleeping with a fan is killing your body, "Detox your liver after decades of wine nights", and "Signs a heart attack is coming", indicating that these reels are designed for virality among an audience in search for a healthier lifestyle, while living a not so healthy one, instead of an audience that is genuinely seeking ancestral wisdom. Another giveaway is that the reels often include inserted AI-generated medical animations, showing dramatic visualizations of internal organs exploding or gushing out toxins, all being described as “warning signs”. Hashtags added to Mama Abeni’s reels include #usa #health #healthy #wellness #healthtips, indicating that the main target audience is comprised of US-based health seekers. Among Mama Abeni’s healing recipes are concoctions like a tea made with garlic, olive oil, and lemon (which supposedly works wonders for eyesight, but is decisively non-African). In short, most of her content has very little to do with African Ancestral healing.

For something to be “ancestral” means for it to belong to, or inherit from, an ancestor or ancestors; those from which one is descended. More precisely, when we speak of “Ancestral wisdom traditions” we generally refer to worldviews and practices transmitted across generations, through oral Indigenous knowledge systems and/or sacred texts of religions and philosophies, that shape communal identity, ethics, and healing. Ancestral traditions are both temporal, as they are built and passed on over time, and relational, as they evolve within community and through intergenerational and interspecies exchange. They are also embodied.

AI, with the depth of human knowledge certain LLM’s contain, can arguably pass as a container for ancestral information, and its knowledge, coming from many sources, could – be it through squinting eyes – be seen as relational. AI can not, however, match the embodied presence of a wisdom keeper who lives in relation and reverence to land and sky, plants and legged creatures. This points to a vital difference between knowledge and embodied wisdom. Austrian scholar of knowledge systems, Alexander Kaiser treats wisdom as a dynamic, relational capacity that unfolds through meaningful life experience, bodily connectedness, and “resonance” with the world. In a 2025 paper, Kaiser and his colleagues argue that AI/LLMs, despite advanced knowledge processing, cannot embody wisdom because they lack deep sense‑making, lived experience, and genuine engagement with an uncertain future.

African religions and healing modalities are, like many other ancestral healing practices, generally based on the interconnectedness of all life. It is understood in these cosmologies that people only exist because of their ancestors’ gift of life and nurturance. Synthetic influencers are, despite attempts to make them look and sound authentic, completely removed from land, life, or ancestral roots. They exist in the isolated territory of digital space, doomed to promote random health-related information.

The degree of randomness, however, varies. In the case of Mama Abeni, the product pushed can be considered quite close to the source: a concoction called Soursop bitters, created by the company Serene Herbs. Founded by 26-year-old Kwadwo Tuffour, this wellness brand specializes in African-inspired herbal supplements. Tuffour started his company on Amazon during the COVID pandemic, and grew it to reportedly $3 million in monthly revenue. The products supposedly promote gut health and detoxification, by way of ancestral Ghanaian remedies.

In an interview with online magazine EBONY, Tuffour mentions how “there’s a deep healing wisdom in African herbalism that has often been overshadowed by Western medicine.” He adds that “The Black American community has historically been underserved when it comes to holistic wellness; we've been handed pills for pain but rarely given roots for restoration.” Tuffour specifically emphasises the holistic approach of ancestral African medicine and how he implements this in his company. According to him, African herbal traditions “honor the body as a system, rather than merely focusing on individual symptoms.” He points out how herbs like Soursop, Black Seed, and Moringa don’t just treat; they cleanse, rebuild, prevent, and restore balance. He concludes that, for this reason, the African tradition offers “more than herbal medicine….it’s ancestral knowledge, and it’s time we bring that power back into our daily lives.”

So what is it that we're seeing on Mama Abeni’s Instagram account? It is easy to classify this content as AI slop generated only to push products, which of course is partially true. Yet in this specific case, there might be a slightly more noble cause. At least the product and company behind it are aimed at bringing African herbalism into the limelight. But does this justify the means? Is it morally acceptable, or even legal, to mislead an audience into thinking the person they’re watching (and perhaps even trusting) is authentic and speaking from a specific tradition? Is it, despite the indirect and commercial connection to African herbalism, not still an exploitation of African ancestral traditions, seeing that most of Mama Abeni’s content is centered around generic Western health advice?

The use of machines to create influencers is nothing new. One of the first was Lil Miquela, the avant-garde virtual influencer introduced in 2016 by a Los Angeles company backed by Silicon Valley money, and who by now has collaborated with fashion labels like Prada, Calvin Klein, and Dior. Similar to the current synthetic ancestral influencers, Lil Miquela is made of pixels. A significant difference is that we always knew she was. There was no attempt at deception.

AI has developed enormously since the birth of Lil Miquela, and a recent (March 6, 2026) New York Times article observes how this technological leap is raising alarm among experts that consumers will unknowingly be duped by deepfakes. The authors refer to a study from earlier this year in The British Journal of Psychology, that found that people consistently overestimate their ability to recognize AI-generated faces. Even those with exceptional face-recognition ability (known as ‘super recognisers’) are fooled by faces produced by AI. This, unsurprisingly, as the study concludes, leaves people vulnerable to “fraud and deception”, a risk which is predicted to intensify as technology improves.

The same NYT article also references Timothy Caulfield, research director of the Health Law Institute at the University of Alberta, who observes that the use of AI has grown specifically in the wellness space, “a crowded but lucrative market where consumers rely on perceptions of authenticity and identity to make buying decisions.” With AI, Mr. Caulfield says, brands can experiment inexpensively with a huge variety of avatars until one works. They can avoid high influencer fees of real humans while targeting wellness seekers.

This brings us to a second, successful synthetic influencer: @mingsanhealing, an adorable elderly Asian monk, supposedly 102 years old, who is mostly depicted in the setting of his allegedly Tibetan monastery. Buddhist artworks adorn the background, and at times we get a view of the Himalayan mountain range through one of the building’s windows. Dressed in ruby red and orange robes, with a bald head and a contagious smile that radiates a stage of enlightenment, this character has accrued 1.3 million followers on Instagram. His bio reads: Ming San | Healing Guide, Ancient wisdom for the mind & body, followed by:

⬇️ The pure beetroot I trust ⬇️

This so-called trusted pure beetroot refers to the product linked in Ming San’s bio, namely capsules made and sold by Beravia Health, an e-commerce site selling beetroot capsules promoted for blood flow, energy, and heart health support. A quick search shows that security scanners like Gridinsoft rate the company low (with a 23-28/100 trust score) due to its young domain age (about 8 months as of early 2026), hidden ownership via privacy services, low traffic, and suspicious content flags. While it has valid SSL and no confirmed malware, reviews highlight overhyped claims, mixed customer service, return issues despite a 90-day guarantee, and scam-like marketing tactics common in supplement sites.

But the bad rating and reviews do not appear to make it to Ming San’s Instagram page. Likes on his posts reach into the thousands, with some reels (like one where he teaches his audience to not speak negatively about oneself, not even jokingly, because the nervous system does not understand humor) having gone viral with over 270K likes. Comments to this particular post include, “You are a gift to my darkness. Thank You.” and “Your posts help change my attitude towards Life”. Most of the monk’s reels offer predominantly spiritual guidance, although in one, Ming San is actually acting as a beetroot evangelist, demonstrating how to make a smoothie from beetroot, turmeric, black pepper, and water. Yes, in a blender. The monk promotes this homemade drink by saying they use it “every morning in the monastery” to help aid good circulation.

Remarkably, and at least at the time of writing, very little in the comments section mentions the suspicion of AI, or the fact that beetroot capsules have nothing to do with Tibetan Buddhism. Among my friends, this monk is well-known. One of them, who recently reposted his content in his stories, told me that when he saw it, he wasn’t sure the person was real, but that the message was good, so he let it pass. Another share, which means a win for the marketeers.

One of the biggest issues with synthetic influencers is that there currently is no mention that this content is synthetic or that it is all effectively an ad. If it were transparent that this was AI-generated and marketing-driven, surely the follower count would be affected. This issue has not escaped legislators. In the US, multiple states have recently passed laws requiring disclosure of AI content, including one in California that requires AI companies to watermark it and another that demands social media companies detect and label it. In December, Governor Kathy Hochul of New York signed the nation’s first legislation explicitly requiring the disclosure of “synthetic performers” in advertisements.

In Europe, the EU AI Act (Regulation (EU) 2024/1689) actively regulates AI-generated social media influencers through mandatory labeling and detection requirements. Article 50 of this Act imposes binding transparency obligations on providers and deployers of generative AI systems, and is supposed to be effective since August 2, 2026, requiring machine-readable markings (e.g., metadata or watermarks) for detectability and clear human-readable disclosures for deepfakes or realistic synthetic content like avatars. While writing this in early September 2026, Ming San’s account still does not mention the use of AI.

Before these laws are implemented, we continue to be taken on rides through uncanny valleys. The Japanese roboticist Masahiro Mori first introduced the term, "uncanny valley" (originally bukimi no tani in Japanese, meaning "valley of eeriness") in his 1970 essay published in the Japanese journal, Energy. He described how human-like robots or figures provoke unease when they approach realism, but fail to fully achieve it. Allan Warburton critiqued the notion of uncanny valley in his by now almost archaic video essay from 2017, arguing that photorealistic computer graphics had "conquered" the valley. Advances in rendering had made digital humans and environments convincingly real, shifting focus beyond mere realism. Warburton reframes the uncanny valley as a "cartographic marker" on a broader map of CGI evolution, where he also places the Frontier (the mainstream of cinematic, industrial CGI: big studios, franchises, and VFX factories operating where anything can be simulated), an area called Beyond the Frontier (the cultural and epistemic edge where hyper‑real CGI fuels post‑truth image distrust, and post‑cinematic fragmented spectacle), and the Wilderness (a lawless, experimental zone where artists reject invisibility and instead foreground CGI’s construction, glitches, labour, and software materiality, or what Warburton calls a “digital grotesque.”). So, in Warburton’s terms, synthetic influencers actually escaped the uncanny valley to move beyond the Frontier. And perhaps, while we wait for new laws to be enforced, this will come to be known as the brief moment in time where synthetic influencers reigned freely.

A final synthetic specimen I’d like to briefly mention here is @icelandicwisdom, a bearded Northern-European gentleman wearing a felt flat cap and a patterned woolen sweater of which the colour changes throughout his reels, but the pattern doesn't. Grimur Björnsson (138K followers on IG) is supposedly an “ Icelandic Farmer, sharing old Northern wisdom” His bio also directs his followers to a not so ancient place, with the conspicuous:

Get my favorite soluble fiber here👇

Grimur is either depicted outside, on a tractor or motorized sleigh (once even in front of an erupting volcano), or in his home apothecary, which is embellished with both an Icelandic and an American miniature flag. One of his most popular reels (over 8000 likes) shows him sitting at an outdoor table, with a frosted landscape in the backdrop, and a bunch of bananas. He asks, in his soothing artificial voice: “Did you know that when you cut the tips off your bananas, they won’t ripen as fast?” In the caption, he prompts: "Comment 'yes’ if you want more videos like this”, which accrued over 2000 comments.

At times, the creators of Grimur’s content tend to get experimental. In one recent reel, Grimur is sitting at his kitchen table on which a dissected human leg is placed. It is not quite a medical prosthetic, not quite an actual leg. Grimur pours an artificial drink made of matcha and lemon down the inside of the leg, releasing big chunks of yellow fat. His commentary is “don’t drink this too often, because it is so effective, people will ask if you’re taking Ozempic.” It is in moments like this that the eeriness of the uncanny valley is felt even beyond the frontier, and I begin to feel an urge to leave a comment saying that I’d never heard of lemons or bananas being indigenous to Iceland.

Our valuable and vulnerable desire for wisdom

The makers and beneficiaries of these synthetic influencers capitalize on the human desire to transcend an often disembodied existence in a fast-paced world, by mimicking ancestral practices that offer a more wholesome and connected way of living, while building a simulation with multiple layers of fake. We are made to believe that these are real people speaking from ancient traditions, and since many people have lost touch with their own ancestral (wisdom) traditions, and/or lack the digital literacy or AI detection skills to identify them as fake, they buy what they see whether the reels convert into views, follows, or actual product purchases, or whether more metaphorically, viewers accept the influencers’ words as valid.

Remarkably, all the synthetic influencers mentioned above display criticisms of consumerism, arguing against the intake of fast food, preservatives, food colouring, and pesticides; things that are quite obviously bad for human health, but which the average Western, and especially American, diet is unfortunately still riddled with. Health seekers hungry to escape an unsustainable lifestyle are targeted with basic lifestyle interventions cloaked in ancestral robes, like Ming San making a “detoxifying footbath” with Epsom salt, apple cider vinegar, and ginger.

The fact that these creations have become so Frankensteinian, poses the question of whether they still refer to anything familiar at all, or whether they are an entirely novel phenomenon; a copy of a thing which no longer has a clear original, because it has deviated so much from the source. In his theory on the Simulacrum, Jean Beaudrillard argues that simulacra evolve through four stages: from reflecting reality, to distorting it, to masking the absence of reality, and finally becoming pure simulation, where the copy becomes more "real" than reality itself, a so-called hyperreality. Information Studies scholar Lars Konzack argues that AI-driven content increasingly operates as autonomous simulations, detached from any real referent, and that this destabilizes traditional notions of authenticity, authorship, and audience perception. Synthetic ancestral influencers hover between stages three and four. The deception lies in masking the absence of reality, while at more experimental moments, this content moves into the space of the hyperreal, leaving a trail of destabilization.

Conclusion

My argument here is not that AI is evil; AI is mostly a tool, and what is created with it depends on the hands in which it is placed, whether it’s slop, genocidal propaganda, or legitimate health advice. AI specialist and consultant Georgia Lewis Anderson reframes AI as Collective Human Intelligence, emphasizing the humanness at the source of this new tool. This reframe clarifies issues around agency and responsibility. If something is artificial, it is easier to other it or blame it (i.e., “it is bad because it is AI”). Seen through the lens of Collective Human Intelligence, it becomes possible to view AI health advice and synthetic influencers as phenomena born from something novel, something collective, and human. These manifestations may be nascent, childlike experiments by humans who (in a generous reading) don’t really know what they are doing, and are just playing with their new tools, or (in a less generous one) by humans who intentionally deceive their audiences by exploiting ancestral wisdom.

In the meantime, what is created through this Collective Human Intelligence, is showing signs of a new form of digital animism. Audiences relate to these influencers as “entities” with perceived wisdom, which blurs the lines between fiction, spirituality, and utility, perhaps similar to a quack being a trickster. The ethical stakes of synthetic health influencers lie mostly in the deceit, but also consequentially in the lack of accountability as there is no source to circle back to. A side-effect of AI-generated figures giving lifestyle or medical-adjacent advice, especially when claiming to speak from ancestral traditions, is a growing confusion about what health, ancestry, and wisdom really mean.

So, what would make applications of this type of technology more holistic? If by holistic, we mean the belief that the parts of something are interconnected and can be explained only by reference to the whole, holistic technology means the kind of technology which is built and applied with an understanding of its effect on its surroundings, including human societies and ecosystems; it is an integral part of this. If this understanding is integrated, it does not make sense to practice exploitation, because exploiting the other ultimately means exploiting the self. It does make sense to practice reciprocity and care.

In terms of the representation of ancestral wisdom in the age of AI, it may be useful to look at other issues concerning Indigenous representation. As the United Nations states, “Indigenous Peoples are inheritors and practitioners of unique cultures and ways of relating to people and the environment. (...) They have sought recognition of their identities, way of life and their right to traditional lands, territories and natural resources for years, yet throughout history, their rights have always been violated. Indigenous Peoples today are arguably among the most disadvantaged and vulnerable groups of people in the world.” They are also the most important keepers of ancestral wisdom.

Representing ancestral wisdom in digital, or whatever form, requires respect for the people that have cultivated and safeguarded this wisdom. It demands respecting their intellectual property rights, their traditions, and their dignity. First and foremost, it requires the keepers and custodians of wisdom to be in charge of how and where they are represented. For digital creators, this means having ethical frameworks in place, and understanding their own role in the digital and natural ecosystem. Creating holistic technology asks us to truly understand that we are part of a bigger whole, and that every drop in the digital ocean causes ripples.

The Or@cle is a prophetic machine with an endlessly changing unknown output; much like a stream. Echo–system

Marlot Meyer

It is vital that we return to collective acts of attuning to ourselves, each other, and our environment in this increasingly polarised society. Tracing back to our early designation of nature and the body as a temple, Echo-system offers a technologically aided embodied ritual experience where the relationship between internal and external, individual and collective is created and a common rhythm and resonance can occur. The Mother Tree

When looking for the form this central creature should take, I immediately considered it becoming some kind of Mother Tree. Trees, to me, have always offered themselves as a sort of portal to tune into a slower, deeper, cyclical way of living and looking at the world. They have wisdom they can teach us – if we’re willing to slow down and listen. As resonant mythological symbols and objects of veneration, trees have played a central role in our shared evolution on Earth. Trees have always been central sites for contemplation, gathering, worship, protection, and celebration. As we are now beginning to learn about the intelligence of a forest, we see that trees communicate underground through an immense web of fungi, at the centre of which lie the Mother Trees: the mysterious, powerful entities that nurture their kin and sustain the forest. Because the breath plays such a fundamental role in this work, and the breath itself is a constant symbiotic exchange with the trees and plants, the symbolic force of the tree acting as the guide and site for this experience made perfect sense.

Symbolic in many pagan traditions as representing the sacred feminine – water is life itself, the sustaining force that nurtures us, both in the womb and beyond. In the work, water acts as a sacred element which resonates and responds to participants’ breathing from within the Mother Tree. Just as breath is a mutual exchange between bodies both human and non-human, there is also a fluid movement of water through and between bodies. The water that’s in us was once and will be again a cloud, a river, the cooling system of a data centre, the dewdrops on a cool winter morning. Through a hydrofeminist lens, we realise we are all porous, leaky, and in-common with all living things through the circulation of water and its composition within each kind of body.

Sensitive System

As a precursor to this work for the Holistic Technology Salon, I hosted a workshop experience with wearable vests titled Sensitive System. Here, the ‘artwork’ and meaning of the work were created through the active listening and sensing experience of the 14 participants. The wearable electronics would measure breath and heart rate, translate these into spatial sound, and individual microphones on the wearables would pick up on the frequency and amplitude of the sound and translate this back to each body in the form of electrical stimulation, essentially creating a feedback loop within and between each body. With this experience, I was trying to reimagine how sensing technologies could collect data and information from bodies without allowing that data to be extracted and removed from the site and the source of that data. Through immediate sonification of data, the information remains in the time and space in which it was measured. Because of the resonant quality of sound as it moves through space and bounces off different materialities and bodies, when it was picked up again by the microphones on the vests, the signal had changed to include this information in it.

This inquiry naturally led me to study traditional Chinese medicine and healing practices. Growing up with a father who practises traditional Korean medicine, East Asian medicine has always been close to me. For the past twelve years, I have worked in my clinic, supporting clients with a wide range of health concerns and body-mind symptoms. Working intimately with clients, I have come to recognise that the body never exists in isolation. Personal symptoms often reflect broader social, environmental, and cultural conditions. Sometimes, by listening carefully to one body, I feel I am also listening to the state of the world.

At the same time, I have also witnessed the profound healing capacity of individuals when they reconnect with their innate intention to heal and open their perception to a more holistic understanding of health and well-being. Our individual health is inseparable from planetary health, and each influences our bodies, our families, our communities, and the natural world.

Witnessing these experiences has moved me deeply. From this movement between the inner and the outer, the micro and the macro, Planetary Acupuncture gradually emerged as a way of bridging art and healing. It has become an inquiry into how we might navigate the crises of our time through embodied wisdom and through recovering our sensory, energetic, and relational nature.

Through this process, I have arrived at three realisations that form the foundation of this project.

1. The Body as Living Technology

The body possesses an innate intelligence capable of sensing, feeling, adapting, healing, and creating. This somatic intelligence cannot be replaced by other external technology. Like nature itself, the body knows how to regulate, balance and heal. Biologically, it is designed for homeostasis—to maintain balance through natural rhythms and cycles of activity and rest, expansion and contraction, birth and decay.

The body is also a continuum across generations. Every one of us comes from another body. We cannot exist without other bodies. Our bodies carry the memories and connections of previous generations while bridging toward future generations.

For countless generations, humanity has survived through the wisdom embedded within the body. Ancient knowledge and lived experiences are carried through our cells, blood, bones, skin, and flesh, allowing life to continue through cycles of birth and death. Planetary Acupuncture reflects this embodied intelligence as a form of holistic living technology through which we may discover new ways of responding to the ecological, social, and existential crises of our time.

2. Art and Healing as One Language

Art and healing are not separate disciplines but interconnected human expressions and wisdom for transformation.

Art expands perception. It opens possibilities beyond familiar structures, invites us to embrace uncertainty, and creates new relationships between people, knowledge, and generations.

Healing is equally creative. It asks us to listen deeply, release identities that no longer serve us, and rediscover our capacity to relate—to ourselves, to others, and to life itself.

To heal is to create.

To create is to care and love.

Both art and healing require vision, courage, and living wisdom.

3. Memory: The Origin of Dance

Dance originated as ritual. It was a sacred language that connected heaven and earth through the moving body. Movement is our first language of communication, a preverbal language that existed before spoken language.

One childhood memory continues to guide my work.

As a child, I had difficulty speaking and learning language until I was seven years old. I spent much of my time in silence. I felt little need to speak and was comfortable simply being in silence. During those years, I was often sent to my grandmother's home in the countryside of South Korea, where I spent countless hours wandering through forests, rice fields, and mountains.

One day, while moving with my eyes closed, I began to feel an immense energy flowing through me. My hands and my body were moved by sensation. When I opened my eyes and continued following these sensations, I found myself moving with the wind, the trees, and the old rocks around me. My body had become the forest, the mountain, and the sky. I felt both completely expanded and deeply present within every sensation of my body. I experienced a profound sense of security, belonging, and power. The dance was intimate and sacred. I was home.

This embodied experience of energy dance has remained the compass of my life's path. Through Planetary Acupuncture, I return to the origin of dance, not simply as performance, but as an intimate and universal language that reconnects us with ourselves, with one another, and with the living Earth.

Screen Guardian

Ginevra Petrozzi

Screen Guardian investigates the role of objects in magical practices, while connecting these historical forms with contemporary technologies. In doing so, the work looks to bring together the power of magical imagery and the mysteries of technology and digital infrastructures. 'Screen Guardian' is an aluminium door depicting a lion as the protector of a computer screen. In Etruria (the area north of Rome, where the pre-Roman population of the Etruscans lived), lions were portrayed on sarcophagi as protectors of tombs. The door enacts an ‘apotropaic' protection from harmful web content through its material heft, by depicting an apotropaic symbol such as the lion and by physically preventing the screen from being fully visible. The door shapes new ways of relating to screens, to web interfaces, but also to one's technologically mediated yet non-technically meaningful being in the world, a particular feature of surveillance capitalism.

Moonblood

Annika Kappner, Lucy Cordes Engelman

Moonblood is an immersive video spell by Annika Kappner and Lucy Cordes Engelman that explores Earth’s cyclicality through the lens of menstruation and ancient mother/goddess worship across the planet. Drawing on ecofeminist and technofeminist thought, the project challenges the linear logic of the Capitalocene while proposing that cycles—birth and death, expansion and contraction, inhale and exhale—might be a transformative framework for living. Guided by the voice(s) of an ancient yet futuristic tech doula, visitors journey through histories of suppressed matrilineal knowledge toward speculative futures rooted in balance and reciprocity. Through moving image, archival material, collective movement, somatic exercises, scent, and sound, the work creates a sensorial environment for embodied reflection. Moonblood ultimately invites participants to reimagine their relationship to nature, technology, and the sacred by embracing the power and wisdom of cyclical existence.

Moonblood is an ongoing iterative project supported by the Stimuleringsfonds and Fonds Kwadraat. Different versions have been shown at affect lab, Creative Europe, WORM Rotterdam, and MU Hybrid Art House.

Artwork

Super Memory 3333

Annika Kappner

Super Memory 3333 is a painting and audio work that reflects on the emergent nature of organic memory in contrast to digital memory, as a procedural form of storage and retrieval. The audio piece explores the nature of memory moving between neuroscience, neuromorphic computing, relationality and cosmology. It explores our changing relationship with memory as technological and scientific advancements and shifts in perception of the world go hand in hand. The accompanying painting translates these questions into a material and sensory process. Combining process-based painting with holographic glitter, acrylic gels, used RAMs and residues from optical computing chips, it explores how perception of material reality is continuously altered through, among other things, light, movement, and sound. The reflective and shifting surfaces make the work unstable: what appears to be a fixed image changes with the position and movement of the viewer, suggesting memory not as static but as something relational, emergent and continuously reconstructed.

The work is being developed as part of an ongoing art-science collaboration with the NL-ECO Consortium, supported by NWO.
