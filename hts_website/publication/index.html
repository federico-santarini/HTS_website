Soft Machines

Issue 01 · Contributions on listening, care and technology

This issue gathers five voices around a shared question: what would technology look like if it were built to be listened to, repaired and returned to?

The contributions that follow differ in tone — essay, field guide, correspondence — but they share an interest in the quiet work that surrounds machines: the maintenance, the attention and the habits of the people who live with them.

Listening Is a Technology

Maren Okafor-Lindqvist

Before there were microphones there were rooms, and before rooms there were bodies learning to hold still long enough to hear.

The ear as an instrument

We tend to describe listening as passive, a kind of receiving that happens to us. But anyone who has sat through a long rehearsal knows that listening is built, tuned and maintained, much like any other apparatus. It has parts: posture, patience, a tolerance for boredom. It has failure modes: distraction, assumption, the urge to name a sound before it has finished arriving.

In this sense the ear is less a window than a workshop. What reaches us has already been shaped by habit, by language, by the acoustics of the places we grew up in. To listen carefully is to become aware of that shaping and, occasionally, to loosen it.

Thresholds

Every listening situation has a threshold, the point at which a sound becomes audible and, a little later, meaningful. A refrigerator hum sits below that threshold until the night it stops. Sensors work in the same way: they are set to ignore the ordinary so that they can report the exceptional, and in doing so they quietly decide what counts as ordinary.

A room is only quiet to those who have agreed on what not to hear.

Ilse Marchetti, Notes on Quiet Machines

Feedback as method

Feedback is usually treated as a problem to be eliminated, the shriek that interrupts the concert. Yet it is also the simplest demonstration that a system is listening to itself. A microphone that hears its own speaker is, for a moment, a small act of attention turned inward.

Working with feedback taught me to treat sensing devices as participants rather than tools. They respond, they drift, they have preferences about distance and temperature. Collaborating with them means accepting a slower tempo and a shared authorship.

A small protocol

The following exercise needs one room, one hour and no equipment.

  1. Sit still for five minutes and write down every sound you can name.
  2. Sit for five more minutes and write down only the sounds you cannot name.
  3. Compare the two lists. Notice which one was harder to make.
  4. Repeat the exercise somewhere else, ideally with someone you do not know well.

The point is not to arrive at a better ear. It is to make visible the work the ear was already doing.

Maren Okafor-Lindqvist is a sound researcher who writes about domestic acoustics and shared attention.

Repair Before Innovation

Tavish Ríos

Every object in this room is, at some level, being kept alive by someone. Most of them are never thanked.

What breaks, and who notices

Culture rewards the moment of invention and forgets the long afterlife of the thing invented. A bridge is celebrated at its opening and ignored for the following century, during which a rotating cast of inspectors, welders and painters keep it standing. Software is no different: for every launch there are thousands of unglamorous hours spent on patches, migrations and quiet fixes.

Repair is therefore a form of knowledge that circulates through the hands more than through publications. It lives in the habit of tapping a casing to hear whether something is loose, or in the knowing way a technician says “it always does that”.

The maintainer’s calendar

Imagine a calendar organised not around launches but around upkeep: the day the filters are changed, the week the archive is verified, the afternoon the cables are relabelled. Such a calendar would make care visible as a recurring rhythm rather than an emergency.

Nothing is ever finished; things are merely cared for, or not.

Anselm Quist, The Long Afterlife of Objects

Mending as a form of attention

To mend something you have to look at it more closely than you ever did while it worked. You discover how it was made, where it was compromised, what its makers assumed about the world. A repaired object carries that attention inside it, like a scar that doubles as a signature.

Three small repairs

  • Re-sole a pair of boots instead of replacing them, and keep the old sole.
  • Document one fix you made this month in a form someone else could follow.
  • Ask the person who maintains your tools what they would change, then listen.

None of this is an argument against invention. It is an argument for giving repair a seat at the same table, and for recognising that most futures will be built from things that already exist.

Tavish Ríos works as a conservator and writes about maintenance cultures, small workshops and the ethics of upkeep.

Soft Infrastructures

Isolde Varga

When people say infrastructure they usually mean cables, pipes and servers. We would like to propose a softer definition: whatever lets a group keep going when nobody is in charge.

The kitchen-table network

A collective’s most reliable infrastructure is often an unremarkable one. A shared spreadsheet, a group chat with a long memory, a standing Thursday dinner where decisions get made without being called decisions. These arrangements are rarely designed, yet they carry weight: they distribute news, absorb conflict and remember who owes whom a favour.

What makes them soft is not that they are weak but that they bend. They survive by being rewritten, informally, by everyone who touches them.

Maintenance without a budget

Soft infrastructures run on goodwill, and goodwill is not renewable by default. The risk is that the same two or three people end up holding the whole structure together, quietly, until they stop. Naming that labour, rotating it and thanking it are modest practices, but they are the difference between a network and a dependency.

What a group can do together is limited by what it is willing to remember together.

Odile Rasmussen, Minutes of a Small Assembly

Designing for slowness

Fast systems optimise for response; soft ones optimise for return. A letter, a recurring meeting, an annual gathering: each makes a promise that someone will come back. When we design tools for collectives we might therefore ask less how quickly something can be sent and more how reliably it can be picked up again.

This also changes how we think about failure. A soft infrastructure is allowed to go quiet for a while. Its test is not whether it never stops, but whether it can be started again by someone new.

Isolde Varga is an organiser and researcher whose work looks at how informal groups sustain themselves over time.

A Field Guide to Machine Moods

Kenji Abara-Holm

Machines do not have moods, of course. But they have patterns, and patterns are what we read as mood in anything that does not speak.

How to use this guide

Observation works best at a distance and over time. Sit near your device without asking anything of it and note what it does when ignored. The entries below describe three common dispositions; none is permanent, and all can be observed in the same machine within a single afternoon.

The Sulk

Typically appears after an update. The interface responds, but with a lag just long enough to feel pointed. Progress bars hover at ninety-nine per cent. Best approached with patience and a cup of tea; pleading makes no measurable difference.

The Hum

A low, contented drone from the fan or the power supply, usually heard late in the evening. It indicates stable operation and an absence of demands. Many people report that the hum becomes audible only once they stop working, which suggests that it has been there all along.

The Glitter

A brief, startling burst of colour or noise, often from a screen that has been left on too long. Harmless, occasionally beautiful, and the one mood that most reliably makes strangers lean in to look.

We name the weather so that we can live with it.

Pilar Oyelaran-Wu, A Dictionary of Small Atmospheres

A note on projection

Reading mood into machines says as much about us as about them. It shows how readily attention turns every steady rhythm into company. The guide does not ask you to stop doing this, only to notice that you are.

Kenji Abara-Holm is a designer and occasional field recordist interested in the everyday behaviour of electronics.

Letters to a Future Interface

Noor Benedetti

These letters were written over one winter, each addressed to a device that does not exist yet and might never be built.

First letter: on touch

Dear Interface, I hope that when you arrive you will have learned something about hands. Ours are not tidy. They are cold in the morning, they hesitate, they press harder when they are anxious. I would like you to treat that as information, not as noise.

I imagine you responding the way a good host does: noticing who has come in and adjusting the room, without announcing it.

To be touched back is the oldest form of being answered.

Marguerite Delacroix-Njoroge, Small Treatises on Contact

Second letter: on forgetting

Dear Interface, please learn to forget. Not everything we tell you is meant to be kept, and some things are only true for an afternoon. A friend who remembers every remark we ever made would be exhausting; a good one lets us change.

If you must keep records, let them fade at the edges like pencil. Let us find them again by accident, years later, and be surprised.

Postscript

I realise I am asking for a great deal: tenderness, discretion, a sense of timing. But we asked as much of the telephone, and of the postal service, and of the neighbour who held our spare key. Each of them, imperfectly, learned.

Noor Benedetti writes essays and short fiction about domestic technologies and the manners we develop around them.
